# Ending Anti-Patterns

Examples-first reference for the specific phrases, structures, and habits that kill the close. Each anti-pattern includes the failure mechanism (why it fails) and the replacement pattern. Auto-rejected at draft time.

This file is reference material for Claude to think with at draft time. Banned phrases never appear in candidates surfaced to the creator.

The companion list of banned transitions lives in `knowledge/transition-patterns.md` Section 4. This file expands the ending-specific anti-patterns and the structural failure modes that aren't single phrases.

---

## 1. Banned phrases (auto-reject)

Every phrase below is auto-rejected at candidate generation. The creator never sees them as options. If they ask for one of these phrases verbatim, refuse and explain.

### B-1. "And finally..." / "And lastly..."

Why it fails: telegraphs the end of the video. Tells the viewer "the value is over, time to leave." Viewers stop watching instead of clicking the end-screen card. Source-backed: this is the golden rule's specific banned phrase.

Replacement: pivot to the next problem. Use BE-1 through BE-8 patterns from `knowledge/transition-patterns.md` Section 3.

### B-2. "Thanks for watching."

Why it fails: closes the video conversationally, like a podcast sign-off. YouTube isn't a podcast, the end-screen card is the next click. "Thanks for watching" wastes the click moment on a courtesy line that adds zero forward pull.

Replacement: end on the Bridge sentence. The viewer doesn't need to be thanked; they need to be pointed.

### B-3. "If you liked this video, please subscribe / smash the like button."

Why it fails: begging. The 3-Part Formula's whole point is no begging. Algorithm rewards retention and click-through, not bare subscribe asks. If the body delivered, the subscribe happens organically; if it didn't, asking won't fix it.

Replacement: confidence in the Bridge. "Watch this next" implies the channel is worth subscribing to. The end-screen card carries the subscribe button without asking for it.

### B-4. "Stay tuned."

Why it fails: broadcast-era filler. Reads as ad-break copy. Streaming-native viewers register it as filler and disengage. Also implies future content the viewer can't access right now, the Bridge needs to point at content that already exists.

Replacement: name what's coming and why it matters. "Watch this next where I show you X" is specific and immediate.

### B-5. "Without further ado..."

Why it fails: pure filler. The phrase exists to fill stage silence. On video, it adds two seconds of nothing. Bonus failure: the close is supposed to pivot forward, not introduce something new.

Replacement: just transition. The Bridge sentence itself signals the move.

### B-6. "Today's video was about..." / "In this video we covered..."

Why it fails: topic-label dressed as recap. Announces a category instead of recapping the transformation. Same failure mechanism as the topic-label hook from `vid-intro`, labels don't move viewers.

Replacement: Pivot with the specific transformation, not the topic category.

### B-7. "I hope you enjoyed today's video."

Why it fails: announces the video is over before the close has done its work. Frames the video as entertainment to be enjoyed, not a tool that delivers a transformation. Drops viewers out of the frame before the Gap can pull them forward.

Replacement: skip the meta-commentary. Move directly into Pivot.

### B-8. "Let me know what you think in the comments."

Why it fails: solicits engagement that doesn't drive watch-time or end-screen clicks. The algorithm rewards retention and click-through-rate; comments are downstream. Asking for comments here interrupts the close's forward pull.

Replacement: nothing. If the video deserves comments, it gets them. The close should drive the next click, not the comment box.

### B-9. "Don't forget to..."

Why it fails: implies the viewer might forget. Tentative framing. The Bridge should sound like the next step is obvious, not optional with a reminder.

Replacement: state the action directly. "Watch this next" not "don't forget to watch this next."

### B-10. "Until next time..."

Why it fails: implies the viewer is leaving the channel until the next upload. Goal of every close is to KEEP them on the channel right now via the end-screen click. "Until next time" hands them an exit cue.

Replacement: skip. End on the Bridge. The next video starts immediately.

### B-11. "Make sure to like and subscribe so you don't miss..."

Why it fails: combines begging (B-3) with a vague threat (might miss content). Reads as desperate. Subscribers come from value delivered, not from instructions.

Replacement: confidence in the Bridge. The end-screen card has the subscribe button.

### B-12. "Hit the bell icon..."

Why it fails: generic YouTube boilerplate. Adds zero specificity to this video. Cluttered with platform jargon.

Replacement: skip entirely. The end-screen card and the channel UI handle the bell.

### B-13. "Catch you in the next one."

Why it fails: friendly podcast sign-off. Same failure as B-2, closes conversationally instead of pivoting to the next click. Implies "see you later," which is exactly the framing the close should reverse.

Replacement: skip the sign-off. End on the Bridge.

## 2. Structural anti-patterns (not single phrases)

These are failure shapes the close can fall into even without using banned phrases.

### S-1. The recap dump

> "So we covered hooks, then we covered intros, then transitions, and finally we wrapped up with endings."

Why it fails: lists every body section instead of pivoting on the transformation. Tells the viewer the video is over and they've gotten the value. They click away.

Replacement: ONE-sentence Pivot that names the transformation, not the chapter titles.

### S-2. The novel-problem Gap

> "But the next problem you have is your tax planning."

Why it fails: the Gap has to point at something the channel actually covers (a video the creator could bridge to). Tax planning is off-channel for a coach/creator. The viewer's brain registers "different topic" and disengages.

Replacement: a Gap that names the problem your next video solves (on-channel, the next logical watch). If the body's lesson doesn't connect to anything in the catalog, the body has a framing problem, restructure the Pivot, don't invent a Gap.

### S-3. The future-promise Bridge

> "I'm planning a video on this. Stay tuned."

Why it fails: viewers can't watch what doesn't exist. By the time the next video drops, this audience is gone. Source-backed rule: never link to a video you haven't made.

Replacement: pick a real, already-published video that solves the Gap. If no such video exists, hold the close as a pure subscribe-pointer (per source rule for first videos / repositioning).

### S-4. The bare CTA

> "Subscribe, like, share."

Why it fails: no Pivot, no Gap, no Bridge. The whole formula collapsed. Generic ask viewers ignore.

Replacement: full 3-Part Formula. Don't ship a close that's just a CTA.

### S-5. The hedge close

> "If you have a moment, you might want to consider checking out the next video. It might be useful."

Why it fails: stacks four hedges in 20 words ("if you have a moment", "might want to", "consider", "might be useful"). Reads as zero confidence. Viewers respond to confidence, every hedge is a 5% drop in click-through.

Replacement: declarative Bridge. "Watch this next." Period.

### S-6. The wind-down Pivot

> "Alright, so to wrap up, hopefully this was helpful."

Why it fails: signals the video is winding down. Slows the energy at exactly the moment the close should be pulling forward. Plus "hopefully this was helpful" is hedge-grade self-doubt.

Replacement: declarative Pivot in the creator's voice. "You now have everything you need to X" or "That's how you Y."

### S-7. The multi-CTA stack

> "Subscribe, like, drop a comment, hit the bell, follow me on Instagram, and check out my course."

Why it fails: six asks in one breath. Viewers given six options pick none. Also pulls viewers off YouTube (Instagram link kills algorithm recommendation per format-planner rules for views goal).

Replacement: one ask. Either Bridge to next video (views goal) OR direct sales/email CTA + Bridge (sales/emails goal). Never six asks.

### S-8. The contradiction Gap

> "But the truth is, hooks don't actually matter."

Why it fails: contradicts the body's lesson. Viewer feels gaslit by 12 minutes of hook teaching followed by "hooks don't matter." Trust burned.

Replacement: Gap reveals the NEXT problem on the same stack, not a retraction of the current lesson.

### S-9. The mid-title-period close

> "That's the system. Watch this next."

Why it fails: missing Gap. Pivot landed, Bridge landed, but no Gap means no urgency to click. "Watch this next" without a named reason is just a request.

Replacement: insert the Gap between Pivot and Bridge. The Gap is what makes the click feel obvious.

### S-10. The over-long close

> [60+ seconds of recap, then a Gap, then a Bridge, then "and one more thing", then another CTA, then a sign-off]

Why it fails: the close runs longer than 60 seconds. Becomes a second video instead of a close. Energy drains. Viewers click the next-video card before the actual Bridge lands.

Replacement: cap the close at 60 seconds (90-150 spoken words). If more needs to be said, it goes in the body, not the close.

## 3. The "and finally" reflex

Watch for this specific pattern: a draft starts to wind down naturally because the creator is tired of writing the script and wants it to be over. The first sign is "and finally" or "to wrap things up" or "in conclusion."

When this shows up in a draft, it's a flag the close hasn't been thought through structurally. The fix isn't to swap the phrase, it's to force the 3-Part Formula:

1. What's the ONE-sentence Pivot? (transformation, not topic)
2. What's the ONE-sentence Gap? (the problem your next video solves, the next watch)
3. What's the ONE-sentence Bridge? (specific next video)

If the answers don't come fast, the close isn't ready. Ask the creator the three questions instead of shipping a wind-down.

---

## 4. Failure mode: voice-correct but structurally broken

A close can pass voice-pressure-test (rhythm matches the reference pieces, no banned phrases, no guardrail refusals) and still be a failed close because the structure is broken.

Voice validates the line-level. Structure validates the close-level.

Both have to pass. The order matters: structure first (Pivot/Gap/Bridge present, no banned phrases), then voice (rhythm, opener, energy match, read-aloud test).

If voice passes but structure fails, the close gets shipped sounding right but converting nothing. End-screen click-through stays at 0.9% instead of climbing to 20%. The whole point of the formula is the structure.

## 5. The draft-time scan list

Run every candidate against this list before the creator sees it. Any hit auto-rejects. The why for each family lives in Sections 1 and 2 above.

- **Winding down:** "and finally", "and lastly", "to wrap this up", "to wrap it up", "to wrap things up", "in conclusion"
- **Signing off:** "thanks for watching", "thank you for watching", "appreciate you watching", "until next time", "see you next time", "catch you in the next one"
- **Begging:** "if you liked", "if you enjoyed", "please subscribe", "smash the like", "smash that like", "make sure to like and subscribe", "hit the bell", "turn on notifications"
- **Broadcast filler:** "stay tuned", "without further ado"
- **Topic-label recap:** "today's video was about", "in today's video", "in this video we covered", "i hope you enjoyed"
- **Comment-bait:** "let me know in the comments", "let me know what you think", "drop a comment", "drop your comment"
- **Hedged reminder:** "don't forget to"
- **Vague gap:** "more to learn"

Plus everything in `knowledge/transition-patterns.md` Section 4 (B-1 through B-13), and em-dashes anywhere (universal hard rule).

## 6. Why this list is short (intentionally)

The list is not exhaustive. It's the high-frequency banned phrases. New patterns will emerge as creators run the skill; capture them through `vid-voice-update` and route additions to this file with a failure-mechanism note.

The principle: each banned phrase must come with WHY it fails. Banning a phrase without the reason creates blind spots. Claude can't generalize from "don't say X" to "don't say similar things." But "don't say X because it's broadcast-era filler that telegraphs the end" generalizes. Claude can spot the broadcast-era pattern in new phrases.
