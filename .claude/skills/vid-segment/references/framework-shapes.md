# Framework Shapes

When the principle uses a creator-owned framework, the framework needs a SHAPE: the visual or structural form the components take. Shape decides whether the audience can hold the framework in working memory or not.

Six shapes. Each shape pairs a worked example with a near-miss. The selection matrix at the bottom is the lookup.

This file is reference material for Claude to think with when the segment pulls from `banks/framework-bank/*.md`. The bank entry's `framework_type` field gives the type (process / categorization / decision-model / mental-model). This file decides the visual/structural shape.

## Why shape matters

A 5-step process drawn as a Venn diagram confuses viewers. A 3-domain comparison drawn as arrows confuses viewers worse. Shape is not decoration. It's the cognitive scaffold the framework rides on. Match the shape to the underlying logic and the framework stays in the viewer's head. Mismatch and they forget it the moment the video ends.

The "three is the example, not the rule" principle holds. Most frameworks land best at 3 components, with 5 as the upper limit. More than 5 and the viewer can't hold the full structure.

---

## Shape 1: Arrows (sequential)

Use when the components happen in ORDER. Step 1 leads to Step 2 leads to Step 3. The order is load-bearing. You can't do Step 3 before Step 1.

**Worked (channel-strategy niche):**

Framework: Title → Thumbnail → Intro

> "Three things decide whether a video gets watched. Title earns the click. Thumbnail confirms the click. Intro earns the watch. In that order. Skip the title and the thumbnail can't save you. Nail the title but flub the thumbnail and the click never happens. The order is the framework."

Why it lands: order is the lesson. The arrows show that skipping any step breaks the chain.

**Worked (sales niche):**

Framework: Discovery Call → Proposal → Follow-up

> "Three steps in every consultative sale. Discovery learns the pain. Proposal frames the solution. Follow-up handles the silence after the proposal goes out. Most agencies skip step 3 and wonder why their close rate sucks."

Why it lands: sequential, each step depends on the previous one's output.

**Near-miss (using arrows for non-sequential components):**

A framework drawn as Step 1 → Step 2 → Step 3, but the three components actually overlap and don't have a strict order. Forcing arrows misleads the viewer about how the framework actually works.

Why it misses: viewer applies them in order, hits the second one, finds out the first doesn't work without the third, gives up. Use Venn or pyramid instead.

## Shape 2: Pyramids / Triangles (stacked, equal weight)

Use when the components STACK (foundation → middle → top) OR when 3 components matter equally and form a balanced shape.

**Worked (creator-positioning niche):**

Framework: NEI Triangle (New / Easy / Inspiring)

> "Three things decide whether a video gets watched all the way through. Does it feel new? Does it feel easy? Does it feel inspiring? Hit two and viewers stay. Hit all three and they finish."

Why it lands: three principles, equal weight. The triangle shape signals balance, since none ranks above the others. The viewer remembers the 3-letter acronym (NEI) and the shape together.

**Worked (foundation-stacking framework):**

Framework: Foundation → Marketing → Sales (pyramid)

> "Most coaches try to fix marketing when the foundation's broken. Foundation is your offer and your audience. Marketing is how you reach them. Sales is the conversation that closes them. You can't sales your way out of a broken foundation. Build the bottom layer first."

Why it lands: pyramid signals layering. The bottom is structural; the top depends on what's underneath. Skipping the foundation = the top falls.

**Near-miss (using pyramid for non-hierarchical components):**

A pyramid showing "Stories / Frameworks / Proof" stacked on top of each other, when in fact the three are interchangeable tools used at different moments.

Why it misses: pyramid implies one is foundational. If the components are peers, use Venn or triangle (equal-weight) instead. Pyramid signals dependency.

## Shape 3: Cycles (loops)

Use when the components REPEAT and improve over time. End of cycle returns to start. System gets better with each loop.

**Worked (testing-and-iteration niche):**

Framework: Fail-As-Fast-As-You-Can (MVP → Test → Measure → Adjust → MVP)

> "Most founders try to build the perfect product. The faster move is to fail fast. Build an MVP. Test it. Measure what worked. Adjust. Then back to MVP. Each loop, you fail smarter."

Why it lands: loop signals "no end state, just better iterations." Viewer understands the framework is an ongoing practice, not a one-time event.

**Worked (content-system niche):**

Framework: Capture → Plan → Write → Publish → Measure → Capture

> "Content systems aren't a sequence. They're a loop. Capture what's worth saying. Plan the piece. Write it. Publish it. Measure what landed. Then capture goes upstream again because measurement reveals new ideas. The loop never ends."

Why it lands: explicitly names the loop nature so viewers don't expect a "done" state.

**Near-miss (cycle for sequential one-shot processes):**

A framework drawn as a cycle (A → B → C → A), but in reality the creator only runs the framework once per project (e.g., "Onboarding new client: A → B → C, done").

Why it misses: cycles imply repetition. If the framework runs once and stops, use Arrows instead.

## Shape 4: Venn Diagrams (overlapping)

Use when the components OVERLAP and the magic happens at the intersection.

**Worked (entrepreneurial-fit niche):**

Framework: Passion + Outcomes + Compensation = Entrepreneurial Sweet Spot

> "Three circles. Passion: what you actually love doing. Outcomes: what you reliably produce for clients. Compensation: what you get paid for. The center is the sweet spot. Outside the center, you get one of three traps. Passion + Outcomes without Compensation = Unrewarded. Passion + Compensation without Outcomes = Unfulfilling. Compensation + Outcomes without Passion = Unethical."

Why it lands: the diagram shows BOTH the sweet spot AND the named failure modes. Viewer sees their own situation in one of the three off-center zones.

**Worked (positioning niche):**

Framework: Audience pain + Creator expertise + Differentiated angle = Iceberg Top

> "Your iceberg top sits at the intersection of three things. The audience's actual pain. The expertise you can credibly own. The angle nobody else is taking. Hit two and you're competing. Hit all three and you own the position."

Why it lands: Venn signals "the magic is in the overlap." Off the overlap = some failure mode the viewer can name.

**Near-miss (Venn for non-overlapping components):**

A Venn drawn for "Title / Thumbnail / Intro": three circles overlapping. But these are sequential, not overlapping. The intro doesn't overlap with the thumbnail; the thumbnail's job is done before the intro starts.

Why it misses: Venn implies "shared zone of influence." Sequential components belong as Arrows, not Venn.

## Shape 5: Funnels (broad → narrow)

Use when the components MOVE the viewer from awareness to action. Wide top, narrow bottom. Each stage filters down.

**Worked (marketing niche):**

Framework: Awareness → Consideration → Conversion → Loyalty

> "Four stages every customer moves through. Awareness: they hear about you. Consideration: they're weighing whether to buy. Conversion: they buy. Loyalty: they buy again and tell a friend. Each stage is narrower because not everyone moves to the next."

Why it lands: funnel shape signals filtering. Viewer understands the math (drop-off rate per stage).

**Worked (content-funnel niche):**

Framework: Reach → Watch → Convert (3-stage funnel)

> "Three things you can optimize on YouTube. Reach: how many people see the thumbnail. Watch: how many click and stay through the intro. Convert: how many take the action you wanted (click, sign up, buy). Each stage is narrower than the last. Optimize them in this order, since fixing convert when reach is broken is rearranging deck chairs."

Why it lands: the order matters AND the narrowing matters; funnel shape carries both meanings.

**Near-miss (funnel for components without filtering):**

A funnel for "Quality / Speed / Price": three things that don't filter from each other.

Why it misses: funnel needs a wide-to-narrow flow. Quality / Speed / Price is a triangle (pick two), not a funnel.

---

## Shape 6: Acronyms

Use when the components SHARE A FIRST LETTER and form a memorable word. The acronym becomes the name viewers recall first.

**Worked (title niche):**

Framework: BENS (Big / Easy / New / Safe)

> "Four letters that decide whether a title gets clicked. B for Big: the promise has to feel substantial. E for Easy: the lift has to feel achievable. N for New: the angle has to feel fresh. S for Safe: the viewer has to trust they'll get value. Hit at least one letter and a title can land. Hit all four and it dominates."

Why it lands: BENS is one syllable, easy to remember. The viewer leaves the video knowing the acronym; they'll recall what each letter means as they re-encounter it. The acronym is the hook for memory.

**Worked (objection-handling niche):**

Framework: F.A.S.T. (Feelings / Acknowledge / Solve / Transition)

> "Four moves on every objection. F: name the feeling behind it. A: acknowledge it's valid. S: solve the underlying concern, not the literal complaint. T: transition forward to the close. F.A.S.T."

Why it lands: action-verb acronym, memorable, reads in the order the conversation flows.

**Near-miss (forced acronym):**

A framework where the components don't naturally share first letters, but the creator forced an awkward acronym anyway. "P.R.O.D.U.C.E.R.S": 9 letters, half of which are filler.

Why it misses: acronyms past 4-5 letters lose the memory advantage. If the components don't naturally form a clean acronym, drop the acronym shape and use a different one (pyramid, triangle, etc.).

## Selection matrix (the lookup)

When the bank entry has a creator-owned framework, ask:

| Question | Yes → use |
|---|---|
| Do the components happen in ORDER? | **Arrows** |
| Do the components STACK (one is foundation for the next)? | **Pyramid** |
| Do 3 components matter EQUALLY? | **Triangle** |
| Do the components REPEAT and improve over time? | **Cycle** |
| Do the components OVERLAP, with magic at the intersection? | **Venn** |
| Do the components FILTER from broad to narrow? | **Funnel** |
| Do the components share a FIRST LETTER and form a word? | **Acronym** |

Multiple YES is normal. Default rank: Arrows (if order matters) > Acronym (if memorable) > Pyramid (if stacking) > Venn (if overlap) > Cycle > Funnel > Triangle.

If NO across the board, the "framework" is probably a list, not a framework. Check the bank entry. If `components:` is just a flat list with no logic, the entry is a checklist, not a framework. Use Show-the-Problem visual demo with on-screen step markers instead.

## How vid-segment uses this file

At Phase 2 (structure pass), when the segment's principle pulls a creator framework:

1. Open the bank entry, read `framework_type` and `components:`
2. Apply the selection matrix to pick the shape
3. Note the shape in the structure draft (e.g. "Principle: framework [[3-part-onboarding-system]] (process → arrows shape)")
4. At Phase 3 (prose pass), name the framework, walk the components in shape order, and place a `> [!note] visual:` callout suggesting the on-screen graphic if the format supports it

If the bank entry has a `shape:` field (rare; most don't), respect it. Otherwise, use the matrix.

## Cross-shape rules

- **Three is the default count.** More than 5 components rarely lands. If the framework has 8 components, the bank entry should be split or some components are subsidiary (note them as sub-items under a parent).
- **Name the shape on screen.** Arrows: "Step 1 → Step 2 → Step 3". Pyramid: literally drawn or stacked. Acronym: letters bold. Make the shape visible.
- **One framework per segment.** Stacking two frameworks in one segment dilutes both. If the segment teaches two frameworks, it should probably be two segments.
- **The shape doesn't replace the lesson.** Pretty diagrams without a clear lesson are decoration. The shape is the scaffold; the words do the teaching.
