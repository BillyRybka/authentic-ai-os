# Post-type: Contrarian

## When to pick it

The idea challenges a belief the audience already holds and replaces it with a sharper one. They are not making a careless mistake. They are following advice that sounds right, and that belief is quietly steering their effort the wrong way. The job is to name the belief, show why it is incomplete, and install the better one with evidence they cannot argue with.

## Structure

1. State the common belief. The version they would nod along to.
2. Show why it is incomplete or wrong. Not a strawman, the real reason.
3. Give the sharper belief. One sentence they can keep.
4. Back it with evidence or a concrete example.
5. Say what changes if they adopt it. Make the payoff specific.
6. Close with a strong takeaway or a clear next move.

## Good example

> Most founders think cardio is what gets them lean.
>
> So they spend 40 minutes on the bike and call it a workout, then wonder why nothing changes after three months.
>
> Cardio burns calories for the hour you do it. Muscle burns them every hour you do not.
>
> One founder I work with cut the bike entirely and ran a 20-minute lifting block three mornings a week. Same total time. Down 11 pounds in ten weeks, and his resting heart rate dropped, not rose.
>
> Stop training to feel tired. Train to change what your body does at rest.
>
> If you have one 30-minute slot a day, spend it under a bar, not on a screen counting calories burned.

Why it works: it names the belief the audience actually holds (cardio equals fat loss), shows the real flaw (cardio only pays during the hour), hands them one sharper sentence to keep, backs it with a specific person and real numbers (11 pounds, ten weeks, same time spent), and closes with a concrete next move. Committed, specific, no hedging.

## Bad example (slop)

> When it comes to fat loss, there is a lot of debate out there about cardio versus weights.
>
> The truth is that both have their place, and what really matters is finding what works best for you and your unique body.
>
> At the end of the day, the best workout is the one you will actually do consistently over time.
>
> A balanced approach that combines a bit of everything tends to deliver the most well-rounded results.
>
> So listen to your body, stay committed, and the results will follow.

Why it fails: it refuses to take a side, which is the whole point of a contrarian post. It hedges ("both have their place," "your unique body"), reaches for platitudes ("the best workout is the one you will do"), and replaces a sharper belief with mush. There is no evidence, no number, no example, nothing the reader has to reckon with. It uses no em-dashes and is still pure slop. Slop is a substance failure, not a punctuation one.
