# Platform: LinkedIn

LinkedIn rewards a clear point of view delivered as a skimmable argument. The reader is scrolling a feed in a work mindset. You have one or two lines before the "see more" cut, so the hook carries everything.

The example below is in the strength-coach niche (busy founders, 30-minute training). It teaches the delivery, not the topic.

## Best for

Authority and business point of view. Frameworks, mistakes, contrarian takes, client patterns, practical strategy. Anything where a sharp argument and a useful takeaway do the work.

## How to adapt the post

- **Hook on line one.** The first line is the whole bet. It shows before the cut. Make it face the reader, a belief they hold or a thing they are doing wrong, and open a gap they have to keep reading to close. Not a character intro, not a warm-up. See `references/hooks.md`.
- **Short paragraphs.** One to three sentences each, white space between them. No walls of text. The eye needs landing spots.
- **One argument.** A LinkedIn post makes one point and supports it. Not a tour of seven.
- **Practical takeaway.** Land on something the reader can use or believe differently by the end.
- **Professional, not stiff.** Talk like a sharp person to one other person. Not a press release, not a hype account.
- **CTA is reflective or discussion-based**, and only if earned. A real question beats "thoughts?"

## Length

Usually 120 to 250 words. A story or a full tactical breakdown can run longer, but every extra line has to earn its place. When in doubt, cut.

## Formatting

- Line breaks between thoughts. Generous white space.
- No hashtag stuffing. One to three genuinely relevant tags, or none.
- No gimmick glyphs or arrow-spam unless the creator's voice actually uses them.
- No em-dashes.

## Best-fit post-types

Contrarian (very strong here), mistake, framework, checklist, comparison, warning, story. Almost everything works because the format tolerates length and structure. Do-this-not-that is the weakest fit; it wants the visual rows a carousel gives it.

## What to avoid

- Burying the hook under throat-clearing ("I've been thinking a lot lately about...").
- Opening on a character or a topic ("Marcus ran an agency...") instead of facing the reader. The story is proof, it is not the hook.
- Transcript-style rambling with no shape.
- Hashtag stuffing and engagement bait.
- Corporate voice and em-dashes.

## Good example (LinkedIn delivery, contrarian)

> Cardio is the worst use of a busy founder's 30 minutes.
>
> Not because cardio is useless. Because it is the easiest thing to skip and the slowest to pay off when you are short on time.
>
> My most time-poor client stopped running and put those 30 minutes into three lifts, twice a week. Six months later he was stronger, leaner, and he had not missed a session.
>
> Strength holds up when your calendar does not. It is short, it is hard to fake, and it compounds.
>
> If your training keeps collapsing the week a launch hits, the problem is not your willpower. It is that you picked the version that needs the most time to work.
>
> What would you protect first if you only had 30 minutes, three days a week?

Why it works: hook on line one, short paragraphs, one argument, a specific client moment instead of a vague claim, a real question as the CTA. No filler, no em-dashes.
