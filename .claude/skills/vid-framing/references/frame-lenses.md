# Frame Lenses

A lens is a want the viewer already has. Framing points the video at one.

The move is small and the whole skill rests on it: **hold the material completely still and change only which want it answers.** Same demo, same story, same numbers, same tool. Eight lenses, eight videos, and the creator picks which one they are making.

This is why a boring topic is not a boring video. Boring means the topic has not been pointed at anything yet.

## The rotation, on one video

Topic: a weekly review that catches problems before they blow up. Mechanism: a checklist and a recurring calendar block, both delivery, both staying out of the frames.

| Lens | The frame |
|---|---|
| Control | A video that shows owners how to decide what next week looks like, instead of finding out on Monday morning. |
| Time savings | A video that shows owners how to get back the hours they lose every week to problems they could have caught on Friday. |
| Familiarity | A video that shows owners how to run a preflight check on their week, the way a pilot runs one before takeoff. |
| Frustration | A video that shows owners how to end the Monday morning fire drill for good. |
| Identity | A video that shows owners how to stop being the one who is always reacting and become the one who saw it coming. |
| Curiosity | A video that explains why their busiest weeks are the ones where the least actually gets finished. |
| Convenience | A video that shows owners how to get ahead of next week in twenty minutes on a Friday, without another system to maintain. |
| Repetition | A video that shows owners how to stop solving the same problem every single Monday. |

Eight videos. One checklist. Nothing about the material changed.

## The eight

### Control

**The want:** stop adapting to the thing. Make it adapt to you.

**Sounds like:** a power shift. They are currently the one bending, tolerating, working around. The frame reverses who sets the terms.

**Fails when** the viewer does not feel subordinate to the thing in the first place. Nobody wants control over a process they already run comfortably, and offering it reads as a problem being invented for them.

### Time savings

**The want:** stop spending hours on this.

**Sounds like:** a quantity of work disappearing. Strongest when the hours are countable and the viewer has felt them.

**Fails when** the task is already fast. Saving four minutes is not a video, and dressing it up as one burns the trust that makes the next promise land.

### Familiarity

**The want:** understand the new thing through one they already know.

**Sounds like:** an analogy carrying the whole frame. The unfamiliar thing becomes a version of something they already use without thinking.

**Fails when** there is no honest analogy available. A forced one is worse than none: the viewer arrives expecting the thing they know, finds something else, and stops trusting the comparison and the video together.

### Frustration

**The want:** kill the one specific thing they hate.

**Sounds like:** a named annoyance, narrow and hot. Not "improve your workflow," but the one recurring irritation they would complain about unprompted.

**Fails when** the annoyance is not widely shared. This lens runs on recognition. If only the creator finds it maddening, the frame lands on an audience of one.

### Identity

**The want:** have the output be theirs, and recognisably so.

**Sounds like:** the gap between what they produce and who they are. Strongest where the work carries their name in public.

**Fails when** the output is private or unattributed. Nobody needs an internal report to sound like them.

### Curiosity

**The want:** find the piece they are missing.

**Sounds like:** a gap in what they already know. They have done the obvious things, so the frame promises the part nobody mentioned.

**Fails when** the gap is fake. This is the lens that manufactures hype, and it is the only one where a weak frame actively damages the channel: the viewer arrives for a missing piece, finds the obvious thing restated, and learns that the promises here are not real. Use it only when the video genuinely contains something the viewer would not have guessed.

### Convenience

**The want:** get the result without it becoming another job.

**Sounds like:** an outcome arriving without a corresponding cost in effort or attention.

**Fails when** setup is genuinely heavy. Promising no extra work over an hour of configuration is a promise the video cannot keep, and the comments say so.

### Repetition

**The want:** never do this particular thing again.

**Sounds like:** finality. Not faster, not easier: done with, permanently.

**Fails when** the task is one-time. Nothing to escape, so the frame has no charge.

Time savings and repetition sit close and are not the same. Time savings is about volume, the hours in aggregate. Repetition is about recurrence, the specific grind of doing the identical thing again. A task can eat one minute and still be worth a repetition frame if it happens forty times a week.

Control and identity sit close too. Control is about who sets the terms. Identity is about whose voice comes out the other end. A video can deliver one without the other.

## Going off the list

The eight are the reliable rotation, not a boundary. When the material clearly points at a want none of them names, use it and say which one it is, so the frame is still traceable to a want rather than to a mood.

Two that come up often enough to expect: **risk** (the thing they are exposed to and have not priced in) and **status** (how this reads to a client, a peer, or a boss). Neither belongs in the standing eight because both misfire easily. Risk framing without a real exposure is fear-mongering, and status framing on an avatar who does not care about perception lands as vanity.

## Near-misses

- **Eight rewordings of one lens.** The most common failure and the hardest to see, because every option reads differently. The check is not "do these sound different," it is "do these answer different wants." Tag each frame with its lens privately. Two tags matching means one frame, written twice.

- **The mechanism smuggled back in.** A frame that names the tool has quietly become a video about the tool. The exception is real and narrow: when the mechanism is itself what people are searching for this week, it belongs at the front. That call gets made at step 1, not here.

- **A frame that describes the contents.** "A video that walks through the five steps of the weekly review" answers what happens in the video. Every lens above answers why anyone would sit through it.

- **A frame written as a line of the video.** "You are not disorganised, you are unprepared" is a spoken line. Frames describe the video from outside it, in third person, so vid-title inherits an argument rather than a headline it has to argue with.

- **A payoff that restates the frame.** The frame is the direction. The core payoff is the one concrete thing they end up holding. If the payoff can be deleted without losing information, it was never written.
