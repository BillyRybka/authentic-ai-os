# Positioning Framework

The Person, Problem, Positioning model. Every other doc in the system depends on this.

## The formula

> "I help [Person] [achieve result] by [solving core problem]."

Three steps:
1. **Identify the perfect buyer (Person).** Who specifically?
2. **Identify their biggest problem they know they have and want solved.**
3. **Write one short statement.** "We solve this problem for this person."

Every video, email, offer, and ad then gets positioned as the solution to that problem for that person. Repeat the same shape across all content.

## Good vs bad: paired examples

Show the creator these pairs. They have to feel the difference before drafting their own.

### Pair 1

**Good:** *"I help women spot, survive, and heal from narcissistic relationships."*

Why it works:
- Person is specific (women, not "people in bad situations")
- Problem is specific and named (narcissistic relationships, not "toxic dynamics" or "difficult partners")
- Result is a verb arc (spot, survive, heal) that mirrors the viewer's actual journey

**Bad:** *"I help people with difficult relationships feel better."*

Why it fails:
- "People" = anyone. Couldn't run a targeted ad, couldn't write a title.
- "Difficult relationships" = any relationship that's hard, so every relationship skill overlaps.
- "Feel better" = you can't see it. Nothing for a viewer to verify they got.

### Pair 2

**Good:** *"I help business owners generate sales, views, and leads using YouTube."*

Why it works:
- Person is specific (business owners, not "creators" or "entrepreneurs")
- Result is a stacked, measurable list (sales, views, leads. Three things the viewer wants)
- Channel (YouTube) is named. The creator owns one platform, not ten

**Bad:** *"I help entrepreneurs grow their online presence."*

Why it fails:
- "Entrepreneurs" is broad. Tech founder, Etsy seller, and Fortune 500 CMO are all "entrepreneurs"
- "Grow online presence" is the phrase every marketing agency uses. Zero differentiation
- No platform commitment means the videos can't specialize

### Pair 3

**Good:** *"I help busy dads get strong and lean using short 20-minute workouts with no gym required."*

Why it works:
- Person has a life role (busy dads) that signals the constraint of time and family demands
- Result is specific (strong AND lean, two named outcomes)
- Method signals the objection it solves upfront (no gym, only 20 minutes)

**Bad:** *"I help people who want to be healthy feel their best."*

Why it fails:
- "People who want to be healthy" = almost everyone alive
- "Feel their best" = you can't see it, you can't measure it
- No Method means the creator competes on the same axis as every fitness channel

### Pair 4

**Good:** *"I help physiotherapists launch premium in-person packages that replace the hourly-rate model."*

Why it works:
- Person has a profession (physiotherapists, not "therapists" or "healthcare workers")
- Problem is named by contrast ("hourly-rate model", the exact trap the viewer is in)
- Result is a specific business pivot (launch premium packages)

**Bad:** *"I help healthcare professionals make more money."*

Why it fails:
- "Healthcare professionals" spans doctors, nurses, chiropractors, dentists. Completely different problems
- "Make more money" is generic. No problem named, no method. Could be 1,000 other channels.

### Pair 5

**Good:** *"I help busy dads lose 30 pounds without giving up pizza."*

Why it works:
- Person is a life role + constraint (busy dads, time-pressed, not gym-bro 20-somethings)
- Result is a specific number (30 pounds). Viewers can verify they got it
- Method directly answers the #1 objection in the niche ("without giving up pizza" = no restrictive diet)

**Bad:** *"I help dads improve their lifestyle through better habits."*

Why it fails:
- "Improve their lifestyle" could mean sleep, money, stress, relationships, fitness. Viewer can't tell what they'd get
- "Better habits" is the marketing phrase every personal-development channel uses
- No number, no constraint, no contrast. Nothing to anchor a video idea against

### Pair 6

**Good:** *"I help freelancers turn their service into a scalable course."*

Why it works:
- Person has a stage marker (freelancers, not agency owners, not employees)
- Problem is named by the trap (trading time for money, hourly service work)
- Result is a specific business pivot (course = scalable revenue without more hours)

**Bad:** *"I help service providers grow their business."*

Why it fails:
- "Service providers" includes plumbers, lawyers, agencies, consultants. Completely different operating models
- "Grow their business" is the generic phrase every business coach uses
- No mechanism named. Could be ads, hiring, automation, productizing, or twenty other things.

### Pair 7

**Good:** *"I help women retire before their sixties using a tried and tested investing system."*

Why it works:
- Person is specific (women, not "investors," not "people")
- Result is concrete and time-bound (retire before sixty, viewer can verify by date)
- Method is a defined system (not "tips" or "strategies", a system implies repeatable steps)

**Bad:** *"I help people achieve financial freedom."*

Why it fails:
- "People" = anyone alive
- "Financial freedom" is the most overused phrase in finance content. Zero differentiation
- No timeline, no method, no demographic. Competing on the most crowded axis in the niche

### Pair 8

**Good:** *"I help busy professionals lose weight sustainably using a habit-based system."*

Why it works:
- Person has a constraint baked in (busy professionals = time-poor, not retirees, not students)
- Result has a quality marker (sustainably, directly addresses the yo-yo dieting objection)
- Method is named and contrasts the niche default (habit-based, not restrictive-diet)

**Bad:** *"I help adults lose weight and get healthy."*

Why it fails:
- "Adults" = 5 billion people
- "Lose weight and get healthy" is the fitness-niche default. No axis of differentiation
- No method, no constraint, no objection answered

## Other examples of good positioning statements

- *"I help women spot, survive, and heal from narcissistic relationships."*
- *"I help business owners generate sales, views, and leads using YouTube."*
- *"I help busy dads get strong and lean using short 20-minute workouts with no gym required."*
- *"I help busy dads lose 30 pounds without giving up pizza."*
- *"I help freelancers turn their service into a scalable course."*
- *"I help business owners lose weight with short workouts."*
- *"I help busy professionals lose weight sustainably using a habit-based system."*
- *"I help women retire before their sixties using a tried and tested investing system."*
- *"I stop men getting rejected using a seven-step process that will impress any woman."*
- *"I help YouTubers make more money with a seven-figure product launch system."*

What makes them work:
- Specific Person (not "anyone who wants to be healthy")
- Specific Result (spot/survive/heal, retire by 60, lose 30 pounds. Observable. Not "be happy")
- Specific Problem or Method (narcissistic relationships, habit-based system, no gym, not "toxic people" or "better habits")
- Note the verb variety: "I help," "I stop," "I show," "I teach". Pick the verb that matches the transformation

## Sharp Angle Pass examples

Use these to learn the move, not to copy the content. The goal is to move from surface problem to sharper positioning by naming the deeper blocker, the desired result, and the enemy or constraint.

### Example 1: Real estate advisor

**Surface problem:** "I need to post more local real estate content."

**Sharper read:** Buyers are overwhelmed and every agent sounds the same. They want to trust someone's judgment before booking a call.

**Strong:** *"I help first-time buyers make confident home decisions before they ever walk into an open house."*

Why it works:
- Person is specific: first-time buyers
- Result is clear: confident home decisions
- Timing gives the angle: before the open house, not after they are already overwhelmed

**Plain:** *"I help homebuyers understand the buying process."*

Why it is only plain:
- True, but no tension
- Could be any agent, lender, or generic real estate channel

**Bad:** *"I help people buy homes."*

Why it fails:
- Person is too broad
- Result is the category itself
- No enemy, constraint, or reason to subscribe

### Example 2: Leadership consultant

**Surface problem:** "Managers need better leadership skills."

**Sharper read:** New managers want performance without becoming the micromanager everyone avoids.

**Strong:** *"I help new managers lead high-performing teams without becoming micromanagers."*

Why it works:
- Person is specific: new managers
- Result is concrete for the role: high-performing teams
- Enemy is named: becoming a micromanager

**Plain:** *"I help managers become better leaders."*

Why it is only plain:
- Not wrong, but too expected
- No stage, no fear, no distinct problem

**Bad:** *"I help leaders unlock their potential."*

Why it fails:
- "Leaders" is broad
- "Unlock potential" is invisible
- Viewer cannot tell what problem gets solved

### Example 3: Nutrition coach

**Surface problem:** "They can't stick to meal prep."

**Sharper read:** They are busy parents who need weeknight meals that keep the family fed without turning Sunday into a second job.

**Strong:** *"I help busy parents feed their family healthy weeknight meals without spending Sunday meal prepping."*

Why it works:
- Person has a life constraint: busy parents
- Result is practical: healthy weeknight meals
- Enemy is specific: losing Sunday to meal prep

**Plain:** *"I help families eat healthier."*

Why it is only plain:
- True but broad
- No time constraint or moment of pain

**Bad:** *"I help people improve their nutrition habits."*

Why it fails:
- Expert language
- No person, no scene, no urgent reason to watch

### Example 4: Freelance-to-course mentor

**Surface problem:** "Freelancers need to scale."

**Sharper read:** They are fully booked and making good money, but every dollar still depends on client hours.

**Strong:** *"I help booked-out freelancers turn their service into a course that sells without adding more client hours."*

Why it works:
- Person has stage: booked-out freelancers
- Result is a specific business pivot: service to course
- Enemy is clear: adding more client hours

**Plain:** *"I help freelancers create online courses."*

Why it is only plain:
- Mechanism is named, but the pain is missing
- Does not say why the course matters

**Bad:** *"I help service providers grow their business."*

Why it fails:
- Too many business types
- "Grow" can mean anything
- No constraint, method, or stakes

### Example 5: Sharpness pattern

Most strong statements include at least three of these five:

- **Person:** a recognizable person or stage
- **Result:** what they want
- **Method or raw material:** what the creator uses to get them there
- **Enemy:** what they refuse to become, create, or tolerate
- **Stakes:** what gets worse if they do not solve it

Weak statements usually name the category. Strong statements name the tension inside the category.

## Real channels that locked positioning

These are real channels that grew by following the Person, Problem, Positioning model. The pattern is identical across niches: ONE focused problem, ONE specific person, repeated across every video.

- A physiotherapist channel went from 400 to 358,000 subscribers in just over a year and made $15,000 in week one of launching a low-ticket product
- A narcissistic-relationships channel hit 40,000 subscribers in a year even though the first 5 videos flopped
- A software-engineer-freedom channel went from a few hundred subscribers to 10K in 90 days, then launched a high-ticket product and a mastermind within a year
- A social-dancing-academy channel made €50,000 in 10 days from one offer
- A drumming channel had 10 weeks of zero sales then suddenly hit £30k, then £70k

The pattern across all of them: ONE focused problem, repeated across every video, for years.

## The Volvo and Red Bull lessons

Positioning is built through repetition on ONE axis, not by competing on many.

**Volvo:**
- Person: families with young kids
- Problem: safety
- Statement: "We make cars that keep families safe"
- 30 years of consistent positioning. They never compete on horsepower. If they did, they'd be against every car company in the world.

**Red Bull:**
- Person: millennials
- Problem: low energy
- Statement: "We make drinks that give millennials energy"
- They never compete on taste. Red Bull tastes like Volvo fuel. Competing on taste would put them against 99% of all drinks.

The lesson: find the axis your competition ignores and own it.

## The "Known for one thing" principle

Goal: become associated with a single word or concept in the viewer's mind. Red Bull = Energy. Volvo = Safety.

Successful niche creators usually become "the X guy" or "the X lady": the ads guy, the narcissist lady, the cheese lady. Being known for ONE thing is the mechanism of growth.

(See "Real channels that locked positioning" above for case studies.)

## What bad positioning does to the business

Three-part breakdown:

1. **Only 1 in 4 videos appeals to a viewer's subscribe reason.** They watch 25% of your content in the 30-day warming window. Not enough to build trust and buy.

2. **YouTube stops testing your videos with that viewer bucket.** Inconsistent engagement breaks the algorithm loop.

3. **Email list is 1/4 as functional as it looks.** A 3,000-person list with bad positioning is functionally 750. Because only 25% of subscribers got content relevant to the problem they came for.

## Case study: the notebook creator

One creator posted a video titled "This pocket notebook is (slowly) replacing my phone". It did 3.9M views.

- Next video: 9,300 views.
- Next video: 2,800 views.
- Later video, back on notebooks: "This pocket notebook is transforming my mental clarity". 167,000 views.

What happened: the viral video's audience came for the notebook/person/problem. The next videos had different topics. The audience didn't come back. When the creator returned to the same person + problem, the audience was there.

A viral video without clear positioning does NOT build a channel.

## If the creator is a professional who never had the problem

Doctors, physiotherapists, consultants who teach something they know but didn't personally struggle with. Borrow a real client transformation instead.

Use:
- A real client problem
- Real client actions
- Real client outcome

Never fabricate. Attribute to the client clearly.

## Walking the creator through positioning

Step 1: define the Problem and Result. Two questions:
1. What's the problem you had (or your ideal client had) that your ideal viewers have now?
2. What's the result you got (or your client got) that your ideal viewers want too?

Step 2: define the Person. (Full avatar in the next stage.)

Step 3: write the Positioning Statement using the formula.

Then refine. Go back and forth until it feels right. Check it against:
- Is the Person specific? (Not "everyone")
- Is the Problem specific? (Not "struggles with life")
- Is the Result specific? (Not "feel better")
- Could you compete on an axis the rest of the niche ignores?

## The MVP principle

This is a minimum viable positioning statement. It will almost certainly need to change as data comes in from published videos. Don't get stuck trying to get it perfect. Lock an MVP, test with 3-4 videos, refine based on what actually resonates.

## Anti-patterns

- Competing on a crowded differentiator (if everyone in the niche says "faster," don't also say "faster")
- Generic Person ("anyone who wants to be healthy")
- Vague Problem ("they're stuck")
- Chasing viral hits without positioning (builds subscribers who won't return)
- Changing positioning every few months (never builds association)

### Failed positioning statements (and why)

These are real shapes of weak statements that fail the gut-check. Use them as a sniff test against any draft.

- *"I help people gain more confidence."* Person is everyone alive. Result you can't see. Could be dating, public speaking, body image, business. Viewer can't tell what they're buying.
- *"I help entrepreneurs grow their online presence."* "Entrepreneurs" includes tech founders, Etsy sellers, agencies, Fortune 500 CMOs. "Online presence" is the most generic agency phrase in marketing.
- *"I help people who want to be healthy feel their best."* Two unobservables stacked. Person is anyone, result is anything.
- *"I help busy men get fit."* "Busy men" is 80% of adult men. No life stage, no constraint, no method. Competes on the same axis as every fitness channel.
- *"I help people replace digital with analogue solutions."* Person undefined. This was the implicit positioning of a creator who got 3.9M views on a notebook video, and 9,300 on the next one. The audience came for the notebook, not the philosophy.
- *"I help service providers grow their business."* "Service providers" includes plumbers and consultants. "Grow their business" is a non-promise.
- *"I help adults lose weight and get healthy."* Default of the niche. No constraint, no method, no number, nothing to verify.
- *"I help small business owners with operational inefficiency."* MBA-textbook framing. Viewers don't search "operational inefficiency". They search "why can't I get through my inbox."
- *"I help creators succeed on YouTube."* "Creators" + "succeed" + "YouTube" are all words 10,000 channels use. No axis owned.
- *"I help women improve their confidence and attract better partners."* Two results stitched together = the creator hasn't picked one. Audience can't form a single association.
