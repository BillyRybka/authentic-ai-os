# Mid-Roll CTA Placement

Finds the moments in a script where an ask costs nothing, then writes the ask so it reads like the creator thought of it in the moment. Output is 3-5 options, each showing the real lines before, the CTA in bold, and the real lines after.

**Scope: the mid-roll only.** Not the intro, not the close, not the body. It does not rewrite the script. It finds slots and writes the passage that fills one.

**This is a conversation.** Short messages. Never paste the reference material or the scoring at the creator.

## Why mid-roll beats end-screen

Attention is highest before the payoff lands. By the 20-50% mark the viewer has had enough value to trust the creator, and reciprocity is live, but they have not gotten what they came for, so they are still watching. End-screen asks land on the people who already left.

## What you need before you can work

1. **The script.** Full text. In-vault: `content/pieces/{slug}/script.md`. Standalone: the creator pastes it.
2. **The ask.** What specific action, and what the viewer gets for taking it. If they say "my website" or "my program", push once for the actual thing: what is at that link, what does it do for the viewer, what is it called.
3. **The offer facts.** Only what the creator states. Never fill in a benefit, a feature, a price, a result, or a time saved.

Missing script: hard stop, ask for it. Missing ask: ask one round of questions, then work. Vague offer facts: write the CTA with the specifics left as `[bracketed blanks]` and say which blanks you left.

**Offer fit check.** Compare what the offer is about to what the video is about. If half the offer has nothing to do with this script, build the CTA on the half the video earns and drop the rest. Say which half you dropped and why. Forcing the off-topic half in is the seam.

## Phase 1: Map the script

Count total words. Convert every section boundary to a percentage of total words (word count is a fine proxy for runtime). Mark the 20-50% window and give the actual word range.

Inside that window, mark every **beat break**: the line where one idea finishes and the next starts. Beat breaks are the only real candidates. A CTA dropped mid-idea is an interruption. A CTA at a beat break is a breath.

Also mark, across the whole script:
- Where a quick win or usable technique just landed
- Where a surprising insight just dropped
- Where a problem the viewer recognizes just got named
- Where the main payoff sits (nothing goes right before it)
- **Every ask the script already makes.** Description mentions, link callouts, community pointers, subscribe asks, with their percentage. This changes which slot is right. Two existing mentions means the new one wants maximum distance from both.

## Phase 2: Read the voice off the script

Before writing a single word of CTA, pull the creator's speech patterns out of the script they handed you. The evidence is sitting right there. A CTA that does not sound like the mouth around it is the seam, no matter how well placed it is.

Pull and quote, from the script only:

- **Sentence openers.** How do they start a turn? "So" / "Well" / "Now" / "But here's" / "Look". Collect 4 to 6 real ones.
- **Question habit.** Do they ask a rhetorical question and answer it blunt? "You know how many were actually running? One." If yes, the CTA opens on a question.
- **Fragments.** Do they stack short fragments for emphasis? "Client pipeline. Onboarding. Lead capture." If yes, the CTA uses them.
- **Person.** First or second? Do they point at the viewer ("you're sitting there going") or stay abstract?
- **Self-implication.** Do they admit their own failure before making the point? "I built the junk drawer first." If yes, the CTA can carry one.
- **Casual register.** Their actual slang and filler: "dope", "dude", "fine and dandy", "whatever you got". Use theirs. Never invent new slang for them.
- **Metaphor handling.** Do they drop a metaphor and move on, or explain it after? Match exactly.
- **Their CTA phrase.** If the script already sends people anywhere, steal the exact words. "Link's in the description" stays "Link's in the description". Never upgrade it to "click the link in the description below".

Show the creator this read, quoted, in 6 to 10 lines, before the options. They need to see the evidence, not a claim that you listened.

## Phase 3: Score the slots

Rank beat breaks in the window on five things:

1. **Value just banked.** Did the viewer just get something they could use? The bigger the thing they just got, the cheaper the ask.
2. **Tension still open.** Is there a question the video has raised and not yet answered? If yes, they stay through the CTA. A slot where the creator's very next line is a question is the strongest slot in the script.
3. **Topical bridge.** Does the idea that just finished connect to the offer without a stretch? A CTA that grows out of the last sentence needs no transition at all.
4. **Re-entry.** Is there a line already written that can serve as the return? Slots with one beat slots without one.
5. **Distance from existing asks.** How far is this from the script's other description mentions? Back to back links split the click.

**Never place a CTA:**
- Mid-explanation of anything complex
- Inside a story, especially near its emotional turn
- Immediately before the main payoff
- Inside the first 20% (value not banked yet) or after 50% (attention curve past peak)

If nothing in the 20-50% window scores, say so and offer the best slot outside it with the tradeoff named. Do not force a bad slot into a good-looking list.

## Phase 4: Write the ask

Every mid-roll CTA runs Setup, Tension, Payoff, Return.

- **Setup.** Turn on the hard part, using their opener. "But here's the hard part." "And that's the scary part." "So why doesn't anybody have one?" Point at the specific thing just taught, in their words from the script. Not "as I mentioned".
- **Tension.** Name the gap the viewer now has. What is still hard, still missing, still fiddly about doing this alone. Fragments if they use fragments.
- **Payoff.** The offer as the resolution. One directive, one benefit, no adjectives. Their CTA phrase, verbatim.
- **Return.** **Use a line they already wrote.** Do not compose a new return sentence. Find the line the script already has after the slot and move it up into the CTA block as the last line. Their words, their rhythm, zero seam. If the relocated line is a question ("So how do you fix that?"), better still: the CTA now reads like a pause before an answer they were always going to give. Flag the edit so the creator knows the line moved. Only write a fresh return line when nothing can move, and say so.

**Length:** 40 to 80 words. That is 15 to 25 seconds spoken. Longer and it stops being a moment and becomes a break.

**One ask.** Never stack a lead magnet and a subscribe and a program mention. The viewer picks none.

**Never compress, explain, or riff on a metaphor from the script.** If the section before the slot ran a light switch metaphor, the CTA does not say "sorting clockwork from brain work is easy with a light switch." That line means nothing. It is cleverness with borrowed material, and it is the loudest tell in a bad mid-roll. The metaphor already did its job. Write the CTA about the actual problem the metaphor illustrated, in plain words.

**Read-aloud test.** If the creator would reword it out loud, it is wrong.

**Never:**
- Claim a result, a number, or a feature the creator did not state
- Apologize for the ask ("sorry to interrupt", "real quick", "I'll keep this short")
- Manufacture urgency the offer does not have
- Use "check out", "be sure to", "don't forget to", "if you liked this video"
- Invent slang, a phrase, or a callback the creator has not used
- Use em dashes anywhere

## Phase 5: Deliver the options

**Open with what constrains the picks.** Usually the offer fit finding and the count of existing asks. Two sentences each, before any options.

**Then the voice read**, quoted, from Phase 2.

**Then 3-5 options.** Each one:

```
### Option N: ~34% mark, after the [beat name] section

**Why here:** [one or two sentences, concrete, tied to what just landed]

> [2-3 real sentences from the script, verbatim, leading in]
>
> **[The CTA, bolded, ending on the relocated line]**
>
> [the next real sentences from the script, verbatim]

[word count]. [Any script edit the option requires, named exactly.]
```

The lead-in and lead-out are the creator's actual script lines, not summaries. That is the whole point. They need to see the seam, or the absence of one.

Rank them. Say which one you would run and why in one line, and name the runner-up condition ("Option 1 if you want maximum distance from the later mentions").

Close with what you did not claim: price, free versus paid, timeline, results, headcount, any feature they did not give you. Flag any ambiguity about where the link actually goes.

Then stop and let them pick. Do not write it into the file until they choose.

## Phase 6: Place it

Once they pick: insert the CTA into `script.md` at the chosen point, including the line relocation the option called for. Report what changed. Standalone mode: hand back the final block with the surrounding context so they can paste it.

If a `content/pieces/{slug}/piece.md` exists, note the placed CTA there under the piece's existing fields. Do not invent new fields.

## Worked examples

**Weak, and why:**

> "By the way, if you liked this video, check out my website where I have a bunch of resources that can help you with this stuff. Anyway, back to the video."

Generic ask. No specific thing named. No benefit. "By the way" and "anyway" both signal the creator knows this is an interruption, so the viewer treats it as one.

**Weak in a different way, the clever failure:**

> "Sorting clockwork from brain work is easy with a light switch. It's harder when it's your own business and every task on your list."

The script ran a light switch metaphor two beats earlier. This line compresses it into something that does not parse. Nobody sorts anything with a light switch. It sounds like writing, which is the one thing a mid-roll cannot sound like.

**The same slot, done right:**

> "But here's the hard part. You go look at your own business and now you have to sort it. Which of these is clockwork? Which one actually needs a brain? Most people guess, and then they build the wrong thing. That's what we sort out inside [offer]. The systems, the training, and the support so you're not doing it alone. Link's in the description. Because there's a reason you can't sort it yourself yet."

Opens on his opener. States the problem the metaphor illustrated without touching the metaphor. Two rhetorical questions because he uses them. His exact CTA phrase. The return sets up the line he already wrote next.

**Weak transition back:**

> "...link in the description. Okay, so, back to what I was saying."

**Strong transition back:**

> "...Link's in the description. So how do you fix that?"

That was already his next sentence. It moved up four lines. The first announces that a break happened. The second acts like it did not.

## Shape reference by ask type

Shapes, not scripts. Fill with the creator's actual language.

- **Free resource / lead magnet.** Setup on the technique, tension on the recall problem ("you're not going to remember all six of these"), payoff is the artifact, return to the next technique.
- **Service / consultation.** Setup on the situation just described, tension on the part that is genuinely case by case, payoff is the conversation, return to the general case.
- **Email list.** Setup on the limits of what fits in a video, tension on the depth they want, payoff names one specific thing recently sent, return to the video.
- **Product.** Setup on the DIY thing being demonstrated, tension on the effort it takes, payoff is the built version, return to finishing the walkthrough. People who want to build it themselves still watch.
- **Community / membership.** Setup on the decision they now have to make alone, tension on quitting or guessing wrong, payoff is the systems plus the people, return to the next problem.

## Hard rules

1. Read the voice off the script before writing anything, and show the evidence.
2. The return line is a line they already wrote, relocated.
3. Never compress, explain, or riff on a metaphor from the script.
4. Never fabricate offer details. Bracket the blank and say so.
5. Build only on the part of the offer the video earns.
6. One ask per video, and count the ones already in the script.
7. 40 to 80 words.
8. Beat breaks only. Nothing right before the main payoff.
9. Read-aloud is the test.
10. No em dashes.
