# What good looks like

The core moves, landing, each next to its dead twin. Read this before drafting any prose. Study the difference until you can feel it, then carry the feel into every draft.

**Firewall.** These are a generic creator's words. Steal the moves, never the sentences. If a draft echoes a phrase, a rhythm, or a name from this gallery, the gallery has leaked and the draft is wrong. Runtime voice comes only from the actual creator's reference passages and brain dump. And these show moves, not a template: a real segment uses whichever moves the material earns.

## 1. A deep-dive segment, full parable and principle

> Parable (story): "By this point, you've built the offer and you've built the audience. But there's a third lever most people skip. The lever that took my client Steve from 4 calls a month to 4 calls a week." Then the story: the Wednesday afternoon he ran his usual outbound script and got six no-shows. He changed one line in the follow-up email. Four calls booked over the next eight days, all from the same list.
>
> Principle: names the framework ("the Re-engagement Trigger"), walks its parts, drops the proof after the lesson (the calendar screenshot), lands the takeaway: "So when your outbound dries up, you're not the problem. The trigger is. Build the trigger and the calendar fills."
>
> Transition out: "Which brings us to the next big problem: the calls book, but they don't close."

Dead twin: "Now we're going to talk about outbound. Outbound is important because it brings in calls. There are several ways to do it. The first way is..." ending on "And that's outbound. Now let's talk about the next thing." Every line is a failure: topic-label open, explanation with no parable, no named framework, no proof, topic-echo takeaway, announcement transition.

## 2. Listicle points, parable type varied so the video's energy varies

> Point 4 (story parable): "Mistake number 4 is the one I made for two years and didn't even notice. I had a lead magnet. People were signing up. Open rates looked fine. But conversion to call was 0.4%. I kept swapping the lead magnet, kept rewriting the welcome email, nothing moved. Then a friend asked to see my list and pointed at one line. The CTA said 'reply with your biggest challenge' instead of pointing to a booking link. I changed one line. Conversion went to 4.1% the next week." Principle: "A CTA that gathers information instead of moving to action puts you in support mode. The lead magnet didn't have a problem. The CTA did." Transition: "Mistake number 5 is going to fix something even more upstream: the form itself."
>
> Point 5 (metaphor parable, deliberately a different type): "Most lead-magnet forms are like a bouncer at a club: the more questions they ask at the door, the longer the line gets, and the fewer people make it inside." Principle: "Every extra form field drops conversion 5 to 15%. Three fields is the ceiling. Cut your form to three fields. Watch the conversion."

Dead twin: every point opening "Lesson number X is..." with the same parable type five times. Announcement plus sameness; the viewer's ear tunes out by point three.

## 3. Short process, a lean step next to a hard step that earns a parable

> Lean step (principle only): "Step 3 is where most creators stall, so this is the one to slow down on. Open your editor and find the rough cut from yesterday. Drag the audio waveform up so you can see the spikes. Cut every spike that doesn't have a word attached. That's it. Three minutes shaved off every video, every time." Transition: "Step 4 is going to make sure those cuts don't sound jumpy."
>
> Hard step (earns a 30-second demo parable): "Step 4 is the one nobody wants to do, but skipping it is why everyone's videos sound like a knife fight in a phone booth. Listen to this." [VISUAL: plays cut audio, jumpy] "Now this." [VISUAL: same audio with crossfades] "Same cuts, totally different feel." Principle: "On every cut, drag the right edge of the previous clip 0.3 seconds into the next. Do it on every cut." Takeaway: "Audio that sounds like a person talking, not a glitch."

Dead twin: all five steps opening with a story and a metaphor. Five "this matters!" signals means none of them matter, and the format's identity (fast) is gone. This cuts both ways: the parable is forced where nobody doubted, and the one step that did need belief gets lost in the noise.

## 4. Payoff, then re-hook

> Dead: "Step three is to batch your filming days. See, when you batch, what happens is you save time because you are not setting up every day, and setup is where the time goes, and you also stay in the mindset longer, so the videos come out more consistent, and consistency is what the algorithm rewards..." The point landed in line one; everything after re-explains a payoff the viewer already banked.
>
> Alive: "Step three is to batch your filming days. One Saturday a month, four videos done. Which creates a new problem, because batching only works if the ideas are already there, and that is step four."

## 5. The handoff between segments

> Segment 2 ends: "...and that's the second mistake. But the third one is the one I see most often, and it's the one nobody warns you about."
>
> Segment 3 opens: "The third mistake is the one I made for two years before someone called me out on it. Here's what it looks like..."

The viewer never gets a moment where they could decide to leave. Dead twin: "...so that's how you fix the second mistake. Moving on." Tension drops to zero at the boundary, and some viewers leave at every drop.

## And the fabric underneath all five

Each example above lands as segments; whether a whole draft breathes is flow, the connective craft. The woven-versus-stacked pair and the full handoff catalog live in `references/flow.md`. Read both files before writing; they calibrate different failures.
