# Carousels

A carousel is a swipe sequence, not a long post cut at the line breaks. Most fail the same two ways: the idea never deserved slides, or every slide is a paragraph. This file is the standard for both, when an idea earns a carousel, and how to build one that pulls the reader through.

These rules are structural and cross-sourced. Where the research quoted engagement multipliers (saves, reach), treat those as directional, not law. Build to the structure, not to a stat.

## First gate: does this idea even earn a carousel?

Carousels expand. A single caption distills. Before building slides, decide which the idea wants.

The test: can you write a clean one-line headline for at least four or five distinct beats, in an order where each beat earns the next? If yes, it is a carousel. If you can only find two or three beats, or the beats reorder with no loss, it is a single caption, and forcing it into slides will read as padding.

**Earns slides:**
- A list of distinct tips or points (each point is a slide).
- A step framework or process (step one to step N, in order).
- A before and after transformation.
- A comparison (this versus that).
- A myth and the correction.
- A case study with real beats (situation, action, result).
- A teardown of something into its parts.

**Stays a single caption:**
- One punchy belief with no sub-points to develop.
- A single personal story or one emotional moment that lives in one breath.
- A time-sensitive announcement where instant clarity beats swiping.

When an idea the creator asked to carousel is really a single story or one belief, do not build the carousel. Default to the caption (plus LinkedIn if they wanted it), tell the creator plainly why a single story hits harder as one continuous caption than as slides, and build slides only if the creator explicitly insists after hearing that. The recommendation is the default, not a footnote. A forced story-carousel comes out as wordy narrative slides, which is the exact failure this gate prevents.

## Build it: decompose, do not slice

You already wrote the core as prose. Do NOT chop it at the paragraph breaks. Rebuild it for the swipe.

1. **Name the one payoff.** Write in one line the single thing the reader leaves with. Every slide pays toward it.
2. **Mark the beats.** Walk the core and mark each distinct beat: a step, a claim, a turn, an example. A beat is a unit of meaning, not a sentence and not a paragraph. One beat is one candidate slide.
3. **Give each slide a road-sign headline (4 to 7 words).** It names the slide's one job. If you cannot write a clean headline for a slide, it has no single idea: merge it or cut it.
4. **Run the standalone-sense test.** Each headline must make sense read out of order, and the set must still form one thread. A slide that only makes sense after the previous slide's trailing clause is a chopped paragraph, not a slide.
5. **Rewrite each slide body for the swipe.** One idea, front-load the takeaway, hold the per-slide limit below. Do not paste the core's sentences.
6. **Sequence to a spine.** Hook, then the problem or promise, then one beat per slide building, then the payoff, then one CTA. Re-order for momentum. The core's prose order is rarely the best swipe order.
7. **Add the pull.** Each slide points at the next. No slide is a dead end.

## The slide (the bloat fix)

- **One idea per slide.** If a slide carries two ideas, it is two slides.
- **Thirty words is the ceiling. Two short sentences is the absolute max.** Three sentences is an automatic fail. A slide is a billboard, not a paragraph. "The steps only existed in his head. His team was capable. They just had nothing to follow." is three sentences and three ideas. Split it or cut it.
- **Road-sign headline, 4 to 7 words**, then a short line or two under it only if needed.
- **Front-load the takeaway.** The point lands before the reader parses the rest.

## The cover slide (slide one)

The cover decides whether anyone sees slide two, so it carries most of the weight.

- **Five to ten words. One idea.** Not a title page, a hook. Apply the full hook standard in `references/hooks.md`: face the reader, open a gap.
- **Make it feel incomplete.** The cover should make slide two mandatory: a partial list ("1 of 5"), a claim that demands the why, a before with no after. If the reader does not need to swipe, the carousel is already dead.

## How many slides (let the beats decide)

Do not pick a number and pad to it. The count falls out of the beats.

- Most teaching or framework carousels land around five to eight slides. Emotional or story-led ones run shorter, three to five. Let the content set it.
- **Merge** a beat that cannot stand on its own headline into the slide next to it.
- **Split** a slide that needs more than two short sentences into two slides.
- **Cut.** Never add a slide to hit a number. The delete test: hide a slide and read the rest. If the through-line survives, that slide was padding. Cut it.

## Coherence (each slide earns the next)

- Slide one opens a loop the carousel has to close.
- The delete test, on every slide: remove it and the argument should break. If it does not, the slide is filler.
- The order is causal or sequential, not a reorderable bag of tips. If the slides shuffle with no loss, it is a list pretending to be an argument. Number it honestly or give it a real sequence.
- One promise, paid off on the last slide. A loop opened on slide one and never closed loses the save.

## The last slide and the caption

- **Last slide: one CTA.** Pick a single action, default to "save this for when you...". Save plus share plus comment plus DM is the most common CTA failure. The reader does nothing.
- **The caption is not the slides.** Its first line complements the cover, it does not repeat it. The caption carries the context, the story, and the nuance the slides could not hold, and ends on the one CTA that matches the last slide. Keep it tight.

## Recycled versus native (the one-line test)

A recycled carousel is a post pasted in and sliced. The tells: walls of text per slide, slides that only make sense in order, headers lifted from the source, no pull between slides, slides that reorder with no loss.

The test: could a reader land on any middle slide, understand it on its own, and still feel pulled to swipe both ways? Native, yes. Recycled, no, because the slide is a fragment that only works mid-paragraph.
