# Post-type: Comparison

## When to pick it

The idea clarifies two things the audience constantly confuses. They use the words as if they mean the same thing, so they make the wrong call. The job is to name both, define each one, and show why mixing them up costs the reader.

## Structure

1. Name the two things.
2. Define each one in plain terms.
3. Why the difference matters to the reader.
4. A concrete example that makes the gap obvious.
5. What to do differently once they can see it.

## Good example

> Founders confuse intensity with frequency. They are not the same thing.
>
> Intensity is how hard a single session is. Frequency is how many times you show up in a week.
>
> The confusion matters because the gym sells intensity and your calendar can only pay for frequency.
>
> A founder I worked with did one brutal 90-minute leg day on Sunday, then nothing for six days because he was wrecked. One session a week, max effort.
>
> We flipped it. Four 30-minute sessions, moderate, spread across the week. Same total work, four times the touches.
>
> His strength climbed in eight weeks. Not because the lifts got harder, because they finally got repeated.
>
> Pick the version you can do four times, not the one that looks impressive once.

Why it works: it names both terms, defines each cleanly, ties the difference to the reader's real constraint, proves it with one named founder and specific numbers, and closes with a decision rule. Specific, committed, no filler.

## Bad example (slop)

> When it comes to fitness, there are a lot of important factors to consider.
>
> Intensity and frequency both play a key role in your overall results and progress.
>
> It is really about finding the right balance that works for your unique situation and goals.
>
> At the end of the day, both matter, and the most important thing is to keep moving forward consistently.
>
> Listen to your body and do what feels right for you on your fitness journey.

Why it fails: it never defines either term or shows the gap between them. It hedges toward "balance," reaches for filler, and lands on a platitude that could describe any topic. It uses no em-dashes and is still pure slop. Slop is a substance failure, not a punctuation one.
