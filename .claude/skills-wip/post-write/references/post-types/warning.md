# Post-type: Warning

## When to pick it

The idea protects the reader from a costly move before they make it. They are about to do something that looks smart and feels productive, and it will set them back. The job is to flag it early, explain why it is tempting, show the real cost, and point them at the safer path while there is still time to switch.

## Structure

1. Name the warning. State the move you are telling them not to make.
2. Say why it looks tempting. Show that you understand the pull, so the reader does not feel judged.
3. Show the real risk or cost. What it actually does, not the version they imagine.
4. Give the safer path. The move to make instead, concrete.
5. Land the principle to remember. One line they can carry forward.

## Good example

> Do not answer a stressful month with more running.
>
> When work blows up, an hour on the treadmill feels like control. You sweat, you check a box, you feel like you did something.
>
> But cardio on four hours of sleep and a 12-hour day is just more stress on a system already maxed out. Your resting heart rate climbs, your sleep gets worse, and by week three you are more wrecked, not leaner.
>
> Trade the run for two heavy lifts and a 20-minute walk. You keep the strength you spent a year building, and the walk actually drops your stress instead of adding to it.
>
> When you are already under load, train to recover, not to punish.

Why it works: it names the exact move (more running under stress) before the reader makes it, validates the pull (cardio feels like control), shows the real cost with specifics (resting heart rate, worse sleep, more wrecked by week three), hands over a concrete swap (two lifts plus a walk), and closes on a principle that sticks. Committed, specific, no hedging.

## Bad example (slop)

> Be careful not to overdo it when life gets stressful.
>
> A lot of people push themselves too hard during busy seasons, which can sometimes do more harm than good.
>
> It is important to listen to your body and find the right balance for your unique situation.
>
> At the end of the day, recovery matters just as much as the work you put in, so try to be mindful of your overall wellness.
>
> Remember to take care of yourself and everything else will fall into place.

Why it fails: it names no specific move to avoid and no real cost, so there is nothing to act on. It hedges ("sometimes," "try to be mindful"), reaches for filler ("at the end of the day," "your unique situation"), and dissolves into wellness platitudes. It could be about anything. It uses no em-dashes and is still pure slop. The vagueness is the failure, not the punctuation.
