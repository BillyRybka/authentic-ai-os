# Post-type: Mistake

## When to pick it

The idea is correcting a behavior the audience is doing wrong. The reader is doing something, it is costing them, and they cannot see it. The job is to name the mistake, show the cost, and hand them the better move.

## Structure

1. Name the mistake clearly. Sharper than the obvious version of it.
2. Say why people do it. Make the reader feel seen, not stupid.
3. Show the hidden cost. What it quietly takes from them.
4. Reframe the better approach.
5. Give one practical fix they can act on.
6. Close with a question or a clear next move.

## Good example

> The mistake is not skipping the gym.
>
> It is treating training like a 90-minute event you have to clear your whole evening for.
>
> So the first week a launch hits, that 90 minutes is the easiest thing to cut, and the habit dies with it.
>
> A workout you can only do on a calm day is not a habit. It is a luxury.
>
> Three lifts, 25 minutes, twice a week. Short enough to survive your worst week, not just your best one.
>
> What does your training look like on your busiest week, not your easiest one?

Why it works: it names the real mistake under the obvious one (the size of the session, not the skipping), shows the specific cost (the habit dies the first hard week), reframes to short-and-survivable, gives a concrete fix (three lifts, 25 minutes), and closes on a question that makes the reader check their own worst week. Specific, committed, no filler.

## Bad example (slop)

> Consistency is one of the most important things when it comes to fitness.
>
> A lot of people struggle to stay consistent with their workouts, and that is completely understandable in today's busy world.
>
> The key is to find a routine that works for you and stick with it.
>
> Remember, something is always better than nothing, and small steps lead to big results over time.
>
> Stay consistent and you will reach your goals.

Why it fails: there is no specific mistake, no real cost, and no concrete fix. It hedges ("completely understandable"), reaches for filler ("in today's busy world"), and leans on platitudes ("small steps lead to big results"). It could be about studying, dieting, or saving money. It says nothing, so it builds no trust. Note that it uses no em-dashes and is still pure slop. Slop is a substance failure, not a punctuation one.
