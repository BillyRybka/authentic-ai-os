# Hooks

The first line is the whole bet. On every platform the reader decides in one line whether to keep going, and most never do. A post can be true, well voiced, and perfectly clean, and still die on a flat opener. This file is the standard for that first line.

Two questions live here. Strength: is the opener strong enough to earn the second line? Variety: across a batch, are the openers different enough that it does not read as a machine? Strength is this file. Variety is the hook rotation library in `references/anti-slop.md`. Strength comes first. A varied set of weak openers is still a set of weak openers.

## What a killer hook does (three jobs, fast)

1. **Creates tension.** It opens a gap between what the reader believes and what might be true. "The most consistent client I train does two sessions a week" signals a challenge to "more is better" before it explains anything.
2. **Makes the stakes obvious.** The reader knows immediately why it matters, usually because a cost is named. Not "consistency matters" but "the program that looks impressive online is the one you quit by week three."
3. **Promises a sharper way to think.** A good hook frames the post, it does not just grab. "Your problem is not motivation. It is that your simple routine has fourteen hidden decisions in it" hands the reader a new lens.

A line that does none of these is a topic label, not a hook. "Here is why training consistency matters" is a label. Cut it.

## Face the reader

Every job above points the same direction: at the reader. A strong hook faces the reader (a belief they hold, a behavior they run, a cost they are paying). A weak hook faces the subject (a character, a topic, a scene) and reads as information.

> Weak (faces the subject): "Marcus ran a small content agency and he was the bottleneck on everything."
> Strong (faces the reader): "You think you cannot hand off the work because you cannot trust your team. That is almost never the real problem."

Same story. The second opens on the reader's own wrong belief, then brings the character in one line later as proof. The story is the evidence, never the doorway. A post that opens "So-and-so did X" has buried the hook in a stranger.

## The two-line shape

Most strong hooks are two beats:

- Line one names the belief or the behavior the reader recognizes.
- Line two turns it: the sharper truth, the hidden cost, or the reframe.

> "You are not the bottleneck because your team cannot be trusted.
> You are the bottleneck because the steps are in your head."

> "A ninety-minute session is not dedication.
> It is the thing you skip the first busy week."

The white space between the two lines is part of the hook. It is where the turn lands.

## Five patterns that work

Each in the strength-coach niche. They teach the move, not the topic.

- **Wrong belief, sharper truth.** "People think they quit training because they lack discipline. They quit because the plan was built for a life they do not actually live."
- **Common behavior, hidden cost.** "Adding a fourth training day to fix inconsistency is how you lose the other three."
- **Specific situation, broader lesson.** "The session you skip when a launch hits is the one telling you the program is too big."
- **Contrast.** "A ninety-minute workout is a hobby. A twenty-five-minute one is a system."
- **Provocative reframe.** "Your missed workouts are not a willpower problem. They are a design problem."

The pattern is the engine. The surface phrasing rotates. "Most people think..." is one surface, and it is the one that goes stale fastest when a batch repeats it, so vary how the belief gets named (You think..., Your X is not..., If you..., Everyone tells you...) while keeping the two-beat shape. Variety of surface, consistency of structure.

## Keep the voice line, just not first

Sometimes the source hands you a phrase that is pure voice but flat as an opener. "People undercharge and it kills them" is a great line and a weak hook, because it states a topic instead of facing the reader. Do not throw it away. Open the reader gap in line one and let the voice line land in line two or three.

> "The rate you are afraid to raise is already doing the damage you are trying to avoid.
> People undercharge, and it kills them, quietly."

You keep the grain and you earn the read.

## Your hook is often buried in your own draft

The sharpest sentence in a post is frequently three or four lines down, where the writer finally said the thing plainly. That sentence is the hook. Before you accept a flat opener, scan the draft for the line a reader would screenshot, and move it to the top. "You are still the bottleneck, now with a dashboard" does more work as line one than as the payoff in paragraph four.

## The reader test

The opener works if the right reader thinks one of these:

- "Wait, that is me."
- "I have never heard it put that way."
- "That is painfully true."
- "I need to see where this goes."

If the best the reader can muster is "ok, a post about training," the line is a label. Rewrite it.

## One idea per line

White space is the rhythm of a social post. A wall of three- and four-sentence paragraphs reads as an essay, and an essay is not a post. Put one idea on a line, especially in the opener. The two-line shape only lands if the two lines are actually two lines.

## The hook check (run on the first line of every version)

A yes to all, or rewrite the opener:

1. **Faces the reader.** About their belief, behavior, or cost, not a character or a topic.
2. **Does at least two of the three jobs:** tension, stakes, sharper lens.
3. **No warm-up.** The actual sharp line, not the sentence before it.
4. **Passes the reader test.** A real reader thinks "that is me" or "I need to see where this goes," not "ok, a post about X."

A first line that fails this is the single biggest reason a good post gets scrolled past. Fix the opener before anything else.
