# Framing Conversation Examples

This reference shows one complete framing session from start to finish, followed by two situations that interrupt the normal rhythm.

These are not scripts to paste verbatim. They are examples of:

* How the conversation should move
* What each response needs to accomplish
* How to distinguish a topic from a frame
* How to define a meaningful Grand Payoff
* How to evaluate competing framing directions
* When the AI should ask a question
* When the AI should make a strategic recommendation
* When the AI should stop discussing the decision and move forward

Write fresh language for the creator and situation in front of you.

Italic notes are for the skill builder.

## Principles demonstrated in this reference

Before reading the example, keep these principles in mind.

### 1. Synthesize before asking

The AI should first organize what the creator has already provided.

Do not make the creator repeat the topic, mechanism, audience, story, or available material in separate questions when those details are already present.

A useful synthesis identifies:

* The raw topic
* The target viewer
* The viewer's underlying want
* The mechanism or vehicle
* The creator's distinctive material
* The strategic tension inside the idea
* Any important gaps that genuinely affect framing

Only then should the AI ask a question.

### 2. Ask only questions that change the work

A question earns its place when the answer will materially change:

* The framing options
* The recommendation
* The Grand Payoff
* The strategic read
* The format
* The intended audience
* The stakes

Do not ask questions simply because they appear in a workflow.

### 3. Translate the creator's instinct before evaluating it

Creators often describe video contents rather than a viewer-facing frame.

For example:

> "I want to walk through my Notion setup."

That is not useless. It contains a real instinct.

The AI should identify the audience desire underneath it, express that desire as a proper frame, and let it compete with other directions.

Do not reject the creator's instinct merely because it is not yet framed correctly.

Do not accept it without strategic evaluation either.

### 4. Rotate across distinct viewer desires

Framing options should not be eight rewordings of the same promise.

Each option should appeal to a meaningfully different audience want, such as:

* Building something
* Avoiding a painful outcome
* Gaining an early warning
* Understanding why something fails
* Reducing effort
* Becoming a certain kind of person
* Making a better decision
* Achieving a result that survives growth

The correct test is not whether the wording sounds different.

The correct test is whether the viewer would click for a different reason.

### 5. Use one canonical payoff term

Use **Grand Payoff** consistently.

Do not alternate between "core payoff," "main payoff," "end payoff," and "Grand Payoff" unless there is a real distinction.

In this process, the Grand Payoff is:

> The specific answer, capability, realization, result, resolution, or transformation the viewer is motivated to stay until the end to receive.

A strong Grand Payoff explains what becomes meaningfully different for the viewer by the end of the video.

It should not merely describe:

* What the creator shows
* What sections the video includes
* What tool appears in the video
* What information is mentioned
* What happens during the video

### 6. Recommend based on strategic advantage

Do not say:

> "I like option six."

Explain why the option has the highest potential.

Useful recommendation criteria include:

* Exclusive material
* Firsthand evidence
* Audience relevance
* Emotional leverage
* Curiosity
* A counterintuitive insight
* A meaningful transformation
* A promise competitors cannot easily copy
* A stronger connection between the creator's experience and the viewer's problem

### 7. Keep the strategic fields distinct

The final strategic read includes several related fields, but each field has a separate job.

**Topic** identifies the broad subject.

**Frame** identifies the specific audience-relevant direction.

**Grand Payoff** identifies what the viewer stays to receive.

**Target** describes the intended viewer's current situation, desire, failed attempts, and immediate friction.

**Transformation** describes the meaningful before-and-after shift the video enables.

**Stakes** explain why the unresolved problem matters and what compounds if nothing changes.

These fields should reinforce one another without repeating the same paragraph in different words.

### 8. Do not manufacture stakes

Some ideas have large consequences. Others do not.

The AI should uncover the real cost, not inflate the idea with unsupported drama.

A modest but credible consequence is more useful than an exaggerated threat the video cannot justify.

### 9. Lock the decision when the evidence is sufficient

When the creator chooses a direction and explains why it matters, move forward.

Do not ask whether they are sure.

Do not reopen discarded options.

Do not force another approval step.

A reasoned choice is the signal that the framing work succeeded.

# A Full Session

## Scenario

The creator has built a Notion dashboard that shows where every client project stands.

They have three pieces of distinctive material:

1. The current dashboard
2. A story about discovering that a deliverable had slipped on the day it was due
3. Three earlier tracking systems that the team stopped updating

They do not have meaningful performance numbers or formal case-study data.

Their audience is small agency owners who want reliable visibility across client work without personally chasing every update.

The creator has not used the Opportunity Finder GPT for this idea, so there are no additional opportunity angles to incorporate.

## Step 1: Synthesize the opportunity before asking

> Here is what I am hearing.
>
> **Topic:** Reliable visibility across client projects.
>
> **Target viewer:** A small agency owner who is tired of being the only person who knows where work stands.
>
> **Underlying audience want:** They want to trust that they can see the truth about every active project without checking several tools or asking the team individually.
>
> **Mechanism:** The Notion dashboard. It gives the video specificity and provides a practical demonstration, but it is not automatically the strongest reason to click.
>
> **Distinctive material:** The current dashboard, the missed-deliverable story, and the three earlier trackers that failed because the team stopped maintaining them.
>
> **Strategic tension:** The interesting idea may not be "how to build a dashboard." It may be that most project trackers fail because they ask people to maintain a second version of work rather than making status a natural result of the work itself.
>
> Before I map the possible directions, is there an angle you are already considering?

*The AI begins by converting the creator's raw material into strategic components.*

*This response demonstrates that the AI understands both the visible topic and the deeper problem.*

*The dashboard is identified as the mechanism rather than automatically being promoted to the promise.*

*The strategic tension is stated as a possibility, not forced as the final answer before the creator has contributed their own instinct.*

*There is one question, and it appears only after the AI has created value.*

*The answer to that question will affect the framing rotation, so the question earns its place.*

## Step 2: Translate the creator's initial angle

**Creator:**

> "I was mostly going to walk people through my Notion setup."

**AI:**

> That gives us a useful baseline.
>
> Underneath "walk people through my Notion setup" is a real viewer desire: some agency owners want a system they can copy and build for themselves.
>
> I will include that direction in the rotation as a proper frame rather than treating it as a description of the video's contents.
>
> Then I will let it compete with directions built around deadline surprises, abandoned systems, owner dependence, team administration, and the reason trackers stop being trusted.

**AI move:** Translate the creator's instinct without rejecting it or automatically adopting it.

*"Walk people through my Notion setup" describes what happens in the video.*

*A frame needs to identify why a particular viewer should care about that walkthrough.*

*The AI extracts the legitimate desire beneath the creator's wording: the viewer wants to build a working system.*

*The creator's direction is preserved, but it must still compete with higher-upside possibilities.*

## Step 3: Rotate the idea across distinct audience desires

> There are several legitimate ways to frame this material.
>
> Your original direction is option one, translated into a viewer-facing promise. The remaining options emphasize different reasons a small agency owner might care about the same topic.
>
> The option names are conversation handles. They are not finished video titles.
>
> ### Option 1: The Build
>
> **Frame:** A video that shows small agency owners how to build one dashboard that gives them a reliable view of every client project from kickoff through delivery.
>
> **Grand Payoff:** By the end, the viewer understands the structure, information flow, and operating rules required to build a usable project command center of their own, rather than merely copying the appearance of someone else's dashboard.
>
> ### Option 2: Stop Chasing the Team
>
> **Frame:** A video that shows small agency owners how to see where every client project stands without messaging individual team members for updates.
>
> **Grand Payoff:** By the end, the viewer knows how to replace recurring status-chasing with a system that surfaces the information they need, allowing them to spend their attention on decisions instead of collecting facts the agency already has.
>
> ### Option 3: Catch the Slip Before the Client Does
>
> **Frame:** A video that shows small agency owners how to detect that a project is drifting before the missed deadline becomes visible to the client.
>
> **Grand Payoff:** By the end, the viewer knows which warning signals reveal that a project is going off course and how to make those signals visible early enough to intervene, recover the work, and avoid a last-minute surprise.
>
> ### Option 4: One Version of the Truth
>
> **Frame:** A video that shows small agency owners how to replace scattered project information with one trusted view of everything currently in motion.
>
> **Grand Payoff:** By the end, the viewer knows how to create a source of truth that remains credible enough for the owner and team to make decisions from it, instead of reconciling conflicting information across several tools.
>
> ### Option 5: Become the Owner Who Always Knows
>
> **Frame:** A video that shows small agency owners how to answer client status questions confidently without beginning an internal investigation every time someone asks where a project stands.
>
> **Grand Payoff:** By the end, the viewer understands how to gain enough reliable project visibility to respond with confidence, protect client trust, and stop feeling exposed whenever a client asks for an update.
>
> ### Option 6: Why Every Tracker Eventually Dies
>
> **Frame:** A video that explains why most agency project trackers are abandoned within weeks, and how to design one whose accuracy is maintained through the work itself.
>
> **Grand Payoff:** By the end, the viewer can identify the design flaw that causes a tracking system to become inaccurate and lose the team's trust, then apply a more durable principle that keeps project status reliable without depending on everyone remembering to provide separate updates.
>
> ### Option 7: Visibility Without More Administration
>
> **Frame:** A video that shows small agency owners how to gain full visibility across client work without creating another reporting process for the team to maintain.
>
> **Grand Payoff:** By the end, the viewer knows how to make project status emerge from actions the team already performs, giving the owner reliable visibility without adding another meeting, form, reminder, or recurring administrative task.
>
> ### Option 8: Build a System That Survives Growth
>
> **Frame:** A video that shows small agency owners how to create a project tracking system that remains reliable as the team, client roster, and number of active deliverables grow.
>
> **Grand Payoff:** By the end, the viewer understands how to design project visibility around durable operating principles, so the system can keep working as complexity increases instead of requiring a complete rebuild at every new stage.
>
> **My recommendation is option 6.**
>
> It has the highest potential because it uses the creator's most defensible material: the three failed trackers.
>
> Many creators can show viewers a polished Notion dashboard. Far fewer can explain what caused three earlier systems to fail, what those failures had in common, and what finally changed.
>
> That gives the video three advantages:
>
> 1. It contains a point of view, not only a setup.
> 2. It allows the creator to use firsthand failure as evidence.
> 3. It gives the viewer a diagnostic insight they can apply before copying any particular tool or template.
>
> In this frame, the dashboard still matters, but its role becomes stronger. It serves as proof of the principle rather than being the entire promise.
>
> Pick the option that creates the strongest reaction for you.
>
> If none of them opens the idea up, tell me what feels absent: a more practical lens, a sharper opinion, a more emotional consequence, a narrower viewer, or another rotation.

### Why these Grand Payoffs work

Each Grand Payoff answers the question:

> What valuable change will the viewer experience by staying until the end?

The payoff is not merely:

* Seeing the dashboard
* Receiving a list of fields
* Watching a walkthrough
* Hearing the creator's story
* Learning that trackers can fail

Each payoff identifies a more meaningful outcome:

* Understanding how to build
* Reclaiming attention
* Detecting project drift
* Establishing a trusted source of truth
* Protecting confidence with clients
* Diagnosing system failure
* Removing administrative burden
* Building for future complexity

*The options rotate across different audience desires.*

*Option one appeals to the desire to build.*

*Option two appeals to relief from recurring interruption.*

*Option three appeals to early warning and risk prevention.*

*Option four appeals to certainty and trust.*

*Option five appeals to the owner's identity and confidence.*

*Option six appeals to understanding a hidden cause.*

*Option seven appeals to reduced effort and team adoption.*

*Option eight appeals to durability and growth.*

*The Grand Payoffs do not repeat the frames word for word. The frame establishes the direction. The Grand Payoff specifies what the viewer gains by following that direction to its conclusion.*

*The recommendation is based on material advantage, not stylistic preference.*

*Option one may be the easiest to execute. Option six has the highest ceiling because it is harder to copy and gives the creator a stronger argument.*

## Step 4: Recognize a meaningful selection

**Creator:**

> "Yeah, six. That is actually the thing I understand that nobody else seems to talk about."

**AI:**

> Good. That is the direction.
>
> Notice what changed: the topic stayed the same, but the viewer promise became more valuable.
>
> The dashboard is no longer the destination. It is evidence for an argument that this creator is especially qualified to make.
>
> I am locking option six and validating it against the framing checklist.

**AI move:** Lock the option and continue.

*A choice with a reason attached is the clearest signal that the rotation worked.*

*The creator has identified a connection between the frame and their unique knowledge.*

*There is no need to ask whether they are sure.*

*The short pattern-recognition statement helps the creator understand the strategic shift without pausing the process for a lecture.*

## Step 5: Run the framing checklist

> ### Framing checklist
>
> **1. Is the creator genuinely interested in this specific angle?**
>
> Yes. The creator's response identifies this as the part they understand that others do not discuss. That is stronger evidence than a reluctant selection.
>
> **2. Will the intended audience care deeply about it?**
>
> Yes. Small agency owners repeatedly experience unreliable trackers, update chasing, deadline surprises, and dependence on information stored in people's heads.
>
> **3. Does the direction evoke a relevant emotional response?**
>
> Yes. It creates recognition, frustration, relief, and curiosity. The viewer recognizes the cycle of introducing a new system, watching it become inaccurate, and returning to manual checking.
>
> **4. Does it create curiosity that cannot be satisfied by a simple search?**
>
> Yes. The viewer is not only asking how to build a tracker. They are asking why previous systems failed, what the failures had in common, and what makes a tracking system remain trustworthy over time.
>
> The frame passes.
>
> I am writing the final strategic read now.

*The AI can evaluate audience relevance, emotional leverage, and curiosity.*

*Creator excitement usually requires direct evidence from the creator.*

*In this case, the creator has already provided that evidence, so another question would be unnecessary.*

*If the creator had said only, "Sure, six is fine," the excitement criterion would remain unresolved. The correct move would be to test another direction or ask what part of the idea they most want to explore.*

## Step 6: Write the final strategic read

> # Final Strategic Read
>
> ## Topic
>
> Reliable project visibility for small agencies using a Notion dashboard as the practical mechanism.
>
> ## Final Frame
>
> A video that explains why most agency project trackers are abandoned within weeks, and how to design one whose accuracy is maintained through the work itself.
>
> ## Grand Payoff
>
> By the end, the viewer can identify the design flaw that causes most project trackers to become inaccurate and lose the team's trust, then apply a durable principle for creating project visibility that stays reliable without depending on everyone remembering to submit separate updates.
>
> ## Target
>
> The target viewer runs a small agency and wants to stop being the only person who knows where client work stands.
>
> They have probably tried to solve the problem before.
>
> They introduced a project board, spreadsheet, dashboard, or new status process. For a few weeks, the system appeared to work. Then someone forgot to update a field, move a card, or record a change.
>
> Once the system stopped matching reality, the owner stopped trusting it.
>
> Because the owner no longer trusted the system, they returned to checking Slack threads, opening several tools, attending status meetings, and asking individual team members for the latest information.
>
> The information exists, but it is fragmented.
>
> The owner can see parts of the truth in different places, yet no single view is dependable enough to make decisions from.
>
> They do not simply want a more attractive dashboard.
>
> They want:
>
> * Confidence that the information is current
> * Earlier warning when work begins to drift
> * Fewer interruptions for the team
> * Less dependence on their own memory
> * The ability to answer clients without chasing updates first
> * A system the agency will continue using after the novelty wears off
>
> Their immediate frustration is status-chasing.
>
> Their deeper frustration is that every attempt to create visibility eventually routes the work back through them.
>
> ## Transformation
>
> Before the video, the viewer thinks reliable project visibility requires the team to maintain a separate reporting layer.
>
> The owner believes the solution is:
>
> * More reminders
> * Better discipline
> * More required fields
> * Another status meeting
> * A stricter process document
> * A more sophisticated dashboard
>
> By the end of the video, the viewer understands that a tracker becomes fragile when its accuracy depends on people remembering to describe work after doing it.
>
> They stop treating status reporting as a separate behavior that must be enforced.
>
> Instead, they learn to design the operating system so project status is created through the same actions that move the work forward.
>
> The transformation is not simply:
>
> > From no dashboard to having a dashboard.
>
> It is:
>
> > From a compliance-dependent tracker that gradually becomes fiction to a work-generated source of truth the owner and team can continue trusting.
>
> The viewer leaves able to evaluate their current system differently.
>
> They can distinguish between:
>
> * Information that appears useful but requires constant manual maintenance
> * Information that is produced naturally by the workflow
> * Fields that create visibility
> * Fields that create administrative burden
> * A dashboard that looks complete
> * A system that remains accurate
>
> The viewer is no longer trying to persuade the team to care more about updating a tracker.
>
> They are redesigning the system so maintaining visibility is not a separate job.
>
> ## Stakes
>
> If the owner continues building trackers that depend on perfect manual compliance, each system is likely to fail through the same pattern.
>
> At first, the tracker appears promising.
>
> Then one or two updates are missed.
>
> The dashboard stops matching reality.
>
> The owner catches an error and loses confidence in the information.
>
> The team senses that the owner no longer trusts the system, so they stop treating it as authoritative.
>
> Usage declines.
>
> Accuracy declines further.
>
> The system becomes irrelevant.
>
> That failure creates more than inconvenience.
>
> Every abandoned tracker makes the next system harder to introduce.
>
> The team learns that new processes are temporary. They become less willing to invest attention in the next rollout because they expect it to disappear like the previous one.
>
> That skepticism causes the next system to fail faster.
>
> With no trusted source of truth, the owner continues gathering information manually.
>
> Every project eventually routes back through them.
>
> They become the tracker.
>
> This creates an operational ceiling.
>
> The owner cannot step away from daily delivery because project visibility disappears when they do.
>
> Team members struggle to take full ownership because the owner remains the central source of context.
>
> Managers cannot make confident decisions because they are working from incomplete or conflicting information.
>
> Deadlines begin to slip quietly.
>
> Risks remain hidden until they become urgent.
>
> The owner discovers problems on the day a deliverable is due rather than when the project first begins to drift.
>
> A simple client question triggers a chain of internal messages:
>
> * Who owns this?
> * What was the latest decision?
> * Did the client approve it?
> * Is the deadline still realistic?
> * Why does the board say something different?
>
> The client experiences hesitation where they expected clarity.
>
> The team experiences interruption where they expected autonomy.
>
> The owner experiences responsibility without reliable control.
>
> Because the visible symptom is inconsistent updating, the owner often reaches the wrong conclusion.
>
> They assume:
>
> * The team is careless
> * People need more accountability
> * The process needs stricter enforcement
> * Another status meeting will solve the problem
> * A longer process document will create compliance
>
> The real problem may be structural.
>
> The system asks people to perform a second administrative job after completing the work itself.
>
> Until that design flaw changes, reminders and stricter rules may create temporary compliance, but they will not create a durable source of truth.
>
> ## Mechanism
>
> The Notion dashboard is the practical demonstration.
>
> It should show how the design principle works in a real operating environment.
>
> It is not the whole reason to watch.
>
> The viewer should understand that the principle can survive even if they use a different project management tool.
>
> This distinction broadens the relevance of the video while preserving the specificity of the creator's actual setup.
>
> ## Distinctive Evidence
>
> The creator has three forms of evidence:
>
> 1. Three previous tracking systems that failed
> 2. A concrete story about discovering a slipped deliverable on the day it was due
> 3. A current dashboard built around lessons from those failures
>
> The failed systems establish credibility because they show the creator did not arrive at the conclusion theoretically.
>
> The missed deadline gives the operational problem a human consequence.
>
> The current dashboard demonstrates the alternative.
>
> ## Recommended Format
>
> Deep-dive.
>
> The frame requires enough room to:
>
> * Establish the familiar failure cycle
> * Show why common fixes do not solve the structural problem
> * Compare the failed trackers
> * Reveal the shared design flaw
> * Explain the new operating principle
> * Demonstrate how the current dashboard applies it
> * Help the viewer evaluate their own system
>
> A short walkthrough would show the result without fully delivering the diagnostic insight promised by the frame.
>
> ## Recommended Goal
>
> Views.
>
> The problem is recognizable to agency owners who may not know the creator yet.
>
> The idea can reach beyond viewers specifically searching for Notion because the central promise concerns trust, visibility, deadlines, and owner dependence.
>
> Notion can remain visible in the packaging when useful, but the frame should not depend entirely on existing interest in the tool.
>
> ## Locked
>
> The title, thumbnail, hook, and structure should now inherit this promise:
>
> > The viewer will discover why project trackers become untrustworthy and learn how to create visibility that stays accurate without separate status work.
>
> Downstream work should not reduce the idea to:
>
> * A generic Notion tour
> * A list of dashboard fields
> * A project management template
> * A productivity setup
> * A broad video about running an agency

### Why the strategic fields are written this way

#### Topic

The Topic remains broad and factual.

It identifies the subject without pretending the subject itself is a compelling promise.

#### Final Frame

The Final Frame selects one audience-relevant direction.

It explains:

* Who the video is for
* What problem it addresses
* What argument or route the video takes

It is written descriptively in the third person.

It is not written as a line the creator would say in the video.

#### Grand Payoff

The Grand Payoff describes the valuable result of staying until the end.

It includes two layers:

1. Diagnosis: the viewer can recognize why trackers fail
2. Application: the viewer can use a stronger design principle

This is more valuable than promising only a dashboard walkthrough.

It also avoids an unsupported absolute such as:

> "The viewer will never need to ask for an update again."

The stronger version promises a durable capability without claiming that all communication disappears.

#### Target

Target does not merely list demographics.

It describes:

* The viewer's current operating reality
* What they want
* What they have already tried
* Why those attempts failed
* What they believe they need
* What they actually need

This gives the frame a precise psychological and practical target.

#### Transformation

Transformation does not say only:

> "The viewer goes from confused to confident."

That language is too broad to guide a video.

The transformation names a change in understanding and behavior:

> From enforcing manual status reporting to designing status as a byproduct of work.

That shift can guide the video's structure, examples, explanations, and conclusion.

#### Stakes

Stakes do not repeat the Target section.

Target establishes the current loop.

Stakes show how the loop compounds:

* Failed systems weaken trust
* Weakened trust lowers adoption
* Lower adoption causes faster failure
* Failure returns visibility work to the owner
* Owner dependence limits delegation and growth
* Hidden risk becomes deadline surprise
* Client confidence is affected
* The owner misdiagnoses the problem and applies ineffective fixes

The final movement names the false diagnosis and the structural cause.

# When the Cost Will Not Come

## Context

A frame has been selected, but the Stakes section reads like one flat sentence.

The AI understands what the viewer wants, but it does not yet know:

* What the unresolved problem costs
* Why the creator cares
* What the creator misunderstood
* How the consequence became visible
* What makes the problem emotionally or strategically meaningful

Do not invent dramatic consequences immediately.

Ask for the creator's lived experience.

Ask one question at a time.

**AI:**

> "What did you believe about this before you figured it out?"

**Creator:**

> "Honestly, I thought onboarding was the boring part. I put all my effort into the sales call, handed the client a folder, and moved on. It took me two years to realize that was exactly where I was losing people."

**AI:**

> "What did that misunderstanding cost you?"

**Creator:**

> "I lost four clients that year and blamed delivery the whole time. It was not delivery. They had already checked out in the first week. I just did not find out until month three."

**AI:**

> That gives us the real stakes.
>
> The failure began in week one, but the consequence did not become visible until month three.
>
> Because the cost arrived much later, you blamed the wrong part of the client experience and spent months trying to improve delivery.
>
> **Target:** A service provider who believes closing the sale is the hard part and treats onboarding as administration rather than the beginning of client retention.
>
> **Transformation:** They stop viewing onboarding as a handoff and begin using it to create clarity, confidence, and early commitment before delivery begins.
>
> **Stakes:** Weak onboarding can quietly damage trust during the first week, while the visible loss appears months later. That delay causes the creator to diagnose the wrong problem, improve the wrong stage, and repeat the same client loss without understanding its source.
>
> **Grand Payoff:** By the end, the viewer can recognize the early onboarding failures that cause clients to disengage before delivery has a chance to succeed, then apply a stronger onboarding approach that builds trust from the beginning.

*Two questions open all four strategic fields.*

*The first question reveals the creator's previous belief.*

*That belief often exposes the false diagnosis.*

*The second question identifies the consequence.*

*The answer provides a measurable cost, a timeline, and a delayed connection between cause and effect.*

*The bill arrives in month three, but the mistake occurs in week one.*

*That distance creates a compelling Stakes structure because it explains why an intelligent person continued applying the wrong solution.*

*Stop asking questions once the material opens.*

*The goal is not to collect the creator's complete history.*

*The goal is to find enough truth to write Target, Transformation, Stakes, and Grand Payoff credibly.*

# When the Rotation Comes Back Flat

## Context

The creator is reacting to a product launch.

The AI rotates the idea through several possible lenses, but every direction collapses into the same promise:

> The product is faster now.

The idea may still be:

* Useful
* Timely
* Searchable
* Relevant to existing viewers
* Worth publishing

However, the available material does not currently contain:

* A meaningful conflict
* A surprising conclusion
* A strong transformation
* Distinctive firsthand evidence
* A large emotional consequence
* A unique argument

**AI:**

> Every version is collapsing into the same promise: the product is faster now.
>
> That is useful, but it means this is currently a reference video rather than a high-tension click video.
>
> There is no strong disagreement to resolve, no major consequence to uncover, and no transformation beyond speed.
>
> There are three honest ways to play it:
>
> 1. Narrow the video to the one part of the launch people are most likely to misunderstand or use incorrectly.
> 2. Let a meaningful number carry the video, but only if you have a real test or comparison.
> 3. Treat it as news, publish quickly, and accept that timeliness is doing more work than the framing.
>
> Which game are you trying to play?

**Creator:**

> "It is news. I want the speed. Film it."

**AI:**

> Locked.
>
> **Topic:** The newly launched product update.
>
> **Final Frame:** A timely explanation of what became faster, where the speed difference is noticeable, and which users will meaningfully benefit.
>
> **Grand Payoff:** By the end, the viewer knows whether the speed improvement will materially change their own workflow or whether the update is unlikely to matter for how they use the product.
>
> **Target:** Existing or prospective users who have seen the launch announcement but do not yet know whether the update deserves their attention.
>
> **Transformation:** They move from knowing that the product is "faster" in the abstract to understanding where the difference appears and whether it changes a practical decision.
>
> **Stakes:** The consequence is modest. Without a clear explanation, the viewer may overestimate the update, underestimate it, or spend time testing a change that is irrelevant to their workflow.
>
> **Format:** News.
>
> **Goal:** Views.
>
> We will prioritize speed, clarity, and practical relevance.
>
> There is no reason to manufacture a dramatic conflict the material cannot support.
>
> Next: `vid-title`.

**AI move:** Lock the news format, keep the stakes proportionate, and continue.

*A flat rotation is evidence, not failure.*

*The AI tested several viewer desires and discovered that the available material does not support a deeper promise.*

*That is useful strategic information.*

*Framing should not kill videos.*

*It should identify the likely ceiling clearly so the creator understands the tradeoff before investing time.*

*The AI offers three viable strategic responses: narrow, quantify, or prioritize timeliness.*

*Once the creator chooses news, the AI stops arguing for a deeper angle.*

*Strategy includes recognizing when speed is the creator's actual priority.*

# When the Creator's Option Is Stronger

## Context

The AI generates several framing directions.

The creator then proposes an option that is more closely connected to:

* Their audience avatar
* Their distinctive evidence
* A stronger emotional need
* A more meaningful transformation
* A question only their material can answer

The AI should not defend its own suggestions.

**Creator:**

> "What about framing it around the fear that the agency falls apart whenever the owner takes a day off?"

**AI:**

> That is stronger than my initial recommendation.
>
> It connects the project visibility problem to a deeper audience need: the owner wants proof that the agency can function without their constant presence.
>
> It also gives the dashboard a more meaningful role. The system is not merely helping them monitor work. It is testing whether the business can operate without using the owner as its memory.
>
> I would promote your direction to the lead option.
>
> **Frame:** A video that shows small agency owners how to build enough operational visibility for the business to keep moving when they are unavailable.
>
> **Grand Payoff:** By the end, the viewer can identify where the agency still depends on information stored in the owner's head, then create a project visibility system that allows the team to make progress without waiting for the owner to return.
>
> The failed trackers can still support the argument by showing why previous attempts did not create real independence.

*The goal is to find the strongest frame, not to prove that the AI's first recommendation was correct.*

*When the creator introduces a superior direction, acknowledge it directly.*

*Position the AI's previous options as additional inspiration, not as competitors that need defending.*

# Near-Misses

## Framing errors

* **Eight rewordings of one desire.** Every option sounds different, but several offer the same underlying result. Evaluate the click reason, not the vocabulary.

* **A topic presented as a frame.** "A video about project management in Notion" identifies a category and a tool, but not a specific direction.

* **A contents description presented as a frame.** "A video that walks through the dashboard build" tells us what happens. It does not explain why a particular viewer needs to watch.

* **Specificity without relevance.** "A Notion dashboard for seven-person agencies" is narrower, but it may still lack a meaningful viewer problem or desire.

* **A frame written as spoken dialogue.** "You do not have a tracking problem. You have a maintenance problem." This may become a useful script line, but it is not a descriptive frame.

* **A title presented as a frame.** "Why Every Tracker Eventually Dies" is a useful handle for discussing an option. It is not the full strategic direction.

* **The mechanism promoted without a strategic decision.** Notion appears at the front of every option merely because it is in the creator's material.

* **The mechanism confused with the promise.** The dashboard becomes the destination rather than the evidence or vehicle.

* **A broad problem with no intended viewer.** "A video about why trackers fail" does not establish whose trackers, in what context, or why the failure matters.

## Grand Payoff errors

* **Renaming without improving.** "Core payoff" is changed to "Grand Payoff," but the sentence still describes only what the viewer sees.

* **A contents-based payoff.** "The viewer sees the creator's dashboard."

* **An information-only payoff.** "The viewer learns about project tracking."

* **A frame repeated as a payoff.** The Grand Payoff restates the frame without explaining what becomes different for the viewer.

* **A shallow outcome when a deeper one is available.** "The viewer can build the dashboard this afternoon" emphasizes speed but misses the more meaningful ability to create a system that remains trustworthy.

* **A vague transformation.** "The viewer feels more confident."

* **An unsupported absolute.** "The viewer never needs to ask for an update again."

* **A payoff with no reason to stay.** The answer is fully revealed by the title or could be resolved by one sentence.

* **A payoff that belongs to a different frame.** The frame promises early warning, but the Grand Payoff promises a faster setup.

* **A payoff that depends on a tool rather than a result.** The viewer receives a Notion template but does not understand how or why the system works.

## Target errors

* **Demographics only.** "Agency owners aged 25 to 45" does not describe the situation the video addresses.

* **A generic pain point.** "They feel overwhelmed."

* **A target with no failed attempt.** The document explains what the viewer wants but not what they have already tried or why it failed.

* **A target written as everyone.** The language becomes so broad that no particular viewer feels recognized.

* **A target defined only by the tool.** "People who use Notion" is less strategically useful than "agency owners who cannot trust their current project information."

* **Target and Stakes repeating each other.** Both sections describe status-chasing without escalating the consequences.

* **Invented audience details.** The AI adds team size, revenue, location, or business stage without support from the creator's avatar or material.

## Transformation errors

* **A tool acquisition mistaken for transformation.** "From not having a dashboard to having one."

* **A vague emotional shift.** "From stressed to confident."

* **A transformation with no change in understanding or behavior.**

* **A transformation that is impossible for one video to deliver.** "The viewer builds a fully autonomous agency."

* **A transformation that ignores the frame's central insight.** The frame is about failed adoption, but the transformation focuses only on aesthetics.

* **A transformation written as a list of content sections.**

* **A transformation that begins and ends in almost the same state.**

## Stakes errors

* **Repeating the immediate pain.** "They waste time asking for updates" appears several times without showing what that cost leads to.

* **Jumping to catastrophe.** A minor workflow problem is presented as a threat to the viewer's entire career.

* **Inventing numbers or consequences.** The AI adds lost revenue, churn, or missed deadlines without evidence.

* **Skipping the causal chain.** The document claims a dramatic consequence without explaining how the initial problem produces it.

* **Ignoring the false diagnosis.** The owner blames team discipline, but the system design is never examined.

* **Using dramatic slogans without earning them.** "They are the tracker" appears before the document establishes how all information routes through the owner.

* **Treating all stakes as emotional.** Operational, financial, relational, reputational, and identity stakes may all matter depending on the material.

* **Treating all stakes as large.** A news or reference video may have a modest but credible cost.

## Conversation errors

* **Asking before synthesizing.** The creator has already supplied the necessary material, but the AI asks them to repeat it.

* **Asking questions that do not affect the next response.**

* **Asking several high-effort questions at once.**

* **Explaining framing for too long before applying it.**

* **Rejecting the creator's initial instinct rather than translating it.**

* **Automatically accepting the creator's instinct without testing alternatives.**

* **Providing options without a recommendation.**

* **Recommending based on personal taste.**

* **Choosing only the safest option.**

* **Writing the final strategic read before the creator picks.**

* **Treating a flat selection as genuine excitement.**

* **Asking for confirmation after a reasoned choice.**

* **Reopening discarded directions after the frame is locked.**

* **Continuing to coach after the strategic decision is complete.**

## Downstream handoff errors

* **Leaving the Grand Payoff implicit.**

* **Saving only a working label instead of the full frame.**

* **Allowing the title to introduce a different promise.**

* **Reducing a diagnostic frame to a generic tutorial.**

* **Handing a title generator a finished title before title exploration begins.**

* **Failing to save the Target, Transformation, and Stakes needed by downstream tools.**

* **Saving several competing frames as though all are active.**

* **Letting the mechanism dominate every downstream decision regardless of the locked promise.**

# Canonical Final Output Format

When a frame is locked, the final response should make the decision easy to find.

Use this structure:

## Final Framed Video Idea

**Topic:**
The broad subject of the video.

**Final Frame:**
The specific, audience-relevant direction.

**Grand Payoff:**
The answer, capability, realization, result, resolution, or transformation the viewer stays to receive.

**Target:**
The intended viewer's current situation, desire, failed attempts, and immediate friction.

**Transformation:**
The meaningful before-and-after shift the video enables.

**Stakes:**
Why the unresolved problem matters and what compounds if nothing changes.

**Mechanism:**
The tool, process, experiment, story, challenge, or demonstration used to deliver the frame.

**Distinctive Evidence:**
The creator's firsthand stories, results, failures, examples, data, access, or material that make the video difficult to copy.

**Format:**
The recommended video format.

**Goal:**
The primary strategic goal.

**Locked Promise:**
One concise sentence that the title, thumbnail, hook, and structure must inherit.

Do not make every section equally long.

Expand the fields that require strategic explanation.

Keep obvious fields concise.

The purpose of the final output is not to create a large document for its own sake.

The purpose is to preserve the strategic decision clearly enough that every downstream step can build on the same promise.
