# Transition Patterns

Fill-in-the-blank transition patterns plus worked examples. Transitions are the cement between sections of a video: intro to first body point, point to point, and final body point to ending CTA. Used by `vid-intro` (step 5 of the 6-part architecture in [[intro-architecture]]), `vid-segment` (between body points), and `vid-ending` (the bridge close into the next-video CTA).

A transition does two jobs at once: signal that the previous beat is done (orientation cue), and forward-hook the next beat by tying it to a viewer pain or payoff. Announcement transitions ("now point two") are dead. Forward-hook transitions ("point two is the one that finally got me consistent leads") earn the next 60 seconds.

The canonical banned phrases live in [[intro-architecture]] step 5. They are repeated and expanded here so every transition skill reads the same source.

This is reference material for Claude to think with, not paste at the creator. Skills read it silently, pick 1-2 candidate patterns, fill the slots from the video's specific material, and surface options.

---

## Section 1. Hook-forward transitions (intro to first point)

The intro-to-body bridge. The intro just promised three things in the Setup. This transition closes the intro and earns the first point by promising a result the viewer cares about. Length: one to two sentences. Never longer.

### Patterns

**HF-1.** To start, I'm going to show you how to [solve big problem the viewer feels].
- Worked: "To start, I'm going to show you how to stop your videos pulling 1k views one week and 100k the next." Names the inconsistency pain, leads with "show," not "tell."
- Worked: "To start, I'm going to show you how to stop losing leads in the gap between a discovery call and a follow-up." Concrete pain for a B2B audience.
- Near-miss: "To start, let me tell you why this matters." Why-it-matters is a teaching cue, not a transition. Viewer feels the lecture starting.

**HF-2.** To start, I'm going to show you how to [reach big result the viewer wants].
- Worked: "To start, I'm going to show you how to land your first paying client inside 30 days, even with zero audience." Specific result, specific timeframe.
- Worked: "To start, I'm going to show you how to add an extra 90 minutes of deep work to your day without waking up earlier." Inversion of the obvious tactic.
- Near-miss: "To start, I'm going to show you how to be successful." Result is the abstract category, not a specific outcome. Doesn't pull.

**HF-3.** But how do you actually do that? Let me show you so you can [big result].
- Worked: "But how do you actually do that? Let me show you so you can stop posting daily and still grow." Frames the next section as the answer to a question the Setup just opened.
- Worked: "But how do you actually do that? Let me show you so you can write a sales page in one sitting instead of five." Promise has a number to anchor it.
- Near-miss: "But how do you do that? Let me show you everything." "Everything" is the empty-promise word. The viewer hears "this is going to be a list."

**HF-4.** So step one is critical if you want to [outcome], and it works like this.
- Worked: "So step one is critical if you want to bring viewers back and turn them into buyers, and it works like this." Stakes plus orientation cue plus open frame.
- Worked: "So step one is critical if you want to keep clients past month six, and it works like this." Niche-specific outcome for an agency or coach avatar.
- Near-miss: "So step one is important, and it works like this." "Important" is hedge-grade. "Critical" plus a named outcome earns the line.

**HF-5.** This first part of my system will [solve big problem / deliver big result].
- Worked: "This first part of my system will stop you from sending cold DMs that get ignored." Specific failure removed.
- Worked: "This first part of my system will get your first three clients without a website." Specific receipt promised.
- Near-miss: "This first part of my system will help with stuff." "Stuff" is the dead noun. Name what.

**HF-6.** Most people fail right here because of [common mistake]. But when you do this next step, [outcome].
- Worked: "Most people fail right here because they pick the format before they pick the audience. But when you do this next step, the format becomes obvious." Names the mistake AND the relief.
- Worked: "Most people fail right here because they price by the hour and never escape the trade. But when you do this next step, your pricing matches the result." Inverts a sacred-cow practice.
- Near-miss: "Most people fail here because they don't try hard enough. But when you do this next step, you'll succeed." Generic mistake plus generic outcome equals zero forward pull.

**HF-7.** Starting with #[1] (or "the first one"), [result the count's first item delivers].
- Worked: "Starting with number one, this is the lesson that finally got me consistent inbound leads instead of chasing every week." List Video-format opener that promises a result, not just an item.
- Worked: "The first one is the truth that took me ten years to admit and three days to implement." Tension framing on a personal-confession list video.
- Near-miss: "Starting with number one." Zero forward pull. Pure announcement. Drops viewers on contact.

**HF-8.** Now you know [setup recap one phrase], let me show you how to [first point's payoff].
- Worked: "Now you know the three pieces this depends on, let me show you how to install the first one in under ten minutes." Recap plus install promise plus speed.
- Worked: "Now you know why morning routines stall, let me show you how to fix the first half of yours today." Half-the-problem framing for a quick-win avatar.
- Near-miss: "Now you know what we're covering, let me show you the first thing." Empty recap. Empty next-point. Both halves are dead.

**HF-9.** [Topic-specific bridge phrase], because [the reason the next section matters].
- Worked: "Right, the foundation has to come first, because every tool you bolt on after this either fits the foundation or breaks it." Logical-progression framing for a system-build video.
- Worked: "OK, before any of the tactics work, this layer has to be built, because the tactics are downstream of it." Structural-priority framing.
- Near-miss: "OK, so anyway, let's get into it." Filler verbs. No reason. Throws away the first 5 seconds of body content.

## Section 2. Segment-to-segment transitions (point to point)

The lifeline of multi-point videos (list videos, deep dives with multiple sections, step-by-step walkthroughs). Every transition between body points has to close the previous beat AND forward-hook the next. List Videos especially die without these. Length: one sentence is ideal, two is the cap.

### Patterns

**SS-1.** Which brings us to the next big problem: [the next point's named pain].
- Worked: "Which brings us to the next big problem: viewers who watch 30 seconds and bounce because the first body shot doesn't match the thumbnail." Names the pain. Makes the viewer want the fix.
- Worked: "Which brings us to the next big problem: a calendar full of discovery calls that never close." Quantified pain (calendar full) with a quantified failure (never close).
- Near-miss: "Which brings us to the next big problem: stuff that goes wrong." "Stuff that goes wrong" is the dead phrase. Name the thing.

**SS-2.** Most people fail right here because [specific mistake]. The next step fixes it.
- Worked: "Most people fail right here because they batch content but never batch ideas, so the batches go stale by week three. The next step fixes it." Names the second-order failure.
- Worked: "Most people fail right here because they treat the audit and the rebuild as one task, and the rebuild stalls. The next step fixes it." Process-design failure named cleanly.
- Near-miss: "Most people fail right here because it's hard. The next step fixes it." "It's hard" is the empty-noun trap.

**SS-3.** Next, I'm going to show you how to avoid [specific bad outcome].
- Worked: "Next, I'm going to show you how to avoid the silent retention killer that lives in minute three of every video." Visual-precision phrasing (silent killer, minute three).
- Worked: "Next, I'm going to show you how to avoid the pricing trap most coaches walk into in year two." Time-stamped failure mode.
- Near-miss: "Next, I'm going to show you how to avoid mistakes." Plural mistakes is the giveaway. One named mistake is the move.

**SS-4.** Next, I'm going to show you how to [specific micro-result].
- Worked: "Next, I'm going to show you how to write a 5-second hook that stops scrolling." Result has a measurable trigger (stops scrolling).
- Worked: "Next, I'm going to show you how to send a follow-up email that books the call within 24 hours." Specific window.
- Near-miss: "Next, I'm going to show you how to do something cool." "Cool" is the AI-tell adjective. Replace with the actual outcome.

**SS-5.** [Point N+1] is going to [result the avatar cares about], even if [common excuse the avatar uses].
- Worked: "Point two is going to get you consistent inbound leads, even if you've been told you have to post seven times a week." Inversion of a frequency-bro default.
- Worked: "Number four is going to drop your acquisition cost by half, even if you've never run an ad before." Skill-floor reassurance.
- Near-miss: "Point two is going to help, even if you don't try hard." "Help" is dead. "Don't try hard" insults the viewer. Both halves wrong.

**SS-6.** That leads us into how to [next point's central action verb plus outcome].
- Worked: "That leads us into how to redesign your end screens so 20% of finishers click through to the next video." Specific number plus specific behavior.
- Worked: "That leads us into how to refactor your week so deep work survives Friday." Niche-specific outcome.
- Near-miss: "That leads us into how to do the next thing." Zero verbs. Zero specifics. Skip.

**SS-7.** Now you know [recap of point N's lesson], let me show you [point N+1's promise].
- Worked: "Now you know your transitions are the lifeline, let me show you the three patterns that work in every list video." Recap is one phrase. Promise has a count.
- Worked: "Now you know why the first hour of the day decides the next eight, let me show you the routine that protects it." Recap plus protective frame.
- Near-miss: "Now you know that, let me show you this." Both halves are pronoun mush. Replace both.

**SS-8.** And the result of doing it that way? [Specific receipt or stat]. Now let me show you the next piece.
- Worked: "And the result of doing it that way? My last client cut their workweek from 60 hours to 22 in four months. Now let me show you the next piece." Receipt earns the next section.
- Worked: "And the result of doing it that way? An end-screen click-through that went from 0.9% to over 20%. Now let me show you the next piece." Hard number receipt.
- Near-miss: "And the result? Pretty good. Now let me show you the next piece." "Pretty good" telegraphs no proof. Skip the receipt entirely or earn it.

**SS-9.** Most creators stop here. The ones who don't get [specific result] do this next.
- Worked: "Most creators stop here. The ones who turn views into buyers do this next." In-group framing without naming a person.
- Worked: "Most coaches stop here. The ones whose clients stay past month six do this next." Niche-specific separator.
- Near-miss: "Most people stop here. The successful ones do this next." Generic in-group. Generic outcome. Both halves dead.

**SS-10.** This next part is the one most people get wrong, and it costs them [specific consequence].
- Worked: "This next part is the one most people get wrong, and it costs them their first 30 subscribers." Specific cost.
- Worked: "This next part is the one most people get wrong, and it costs them their second renewal." Account-management consequence.
- Near-miss: "This next part is the one most people get wrong, and it costs them a lot." "A lot" is the dead quantifier. Name the cost.

**SS-11.** "What happened next completely [result], so now I'll show you how to do the same."
- Worked: "What happened next completely changed how I price my services, so now I'll show you how to do the same." Personal-receipt setup for the next teach beat.
- Worked: "What happened next completely flipped the math on lead cost, so now I'll show you how to do the same." Math-flip framing for marketers.
- Near-miss: "What happened next completely changed everything, so now I'll show you how." "Everything" is the empty receipt. Name what changed.

**SS-12.** "To prove this point, let me show you [example or demo]."
- Worked: "To prove this point, let me show you the actual analytics from a video where I tested both versions back to back." Demo as proof.
- Worked: "To prove this point, let me show you the inbox screenshot from the week we changed one line of the welcome email." Receipt as proof.
- Near-miss: "To prove this point, let me show you a thing." Demo without naming the thing tells the viewer "I'm winging this."

## Section 3. Body-to-ending transitions (final point to CTA)

The pivot out of the body and into the ending. Different shape from segment-to-segment because it has to do three things in one breath: close the body cleanly, recap the transformation in a phrase, and reveal the next problem the viewer now has. The next-video CTA lands AFTER this transition. The transition is the bridge to the CTA, not the CTA itself.

The golden rule from [[intro-architecture]] applies double here: never end a video. Don't say "and finally." Don't recap for 30 seconds. Pivot, name the new problem, point to the next video.

### Patterns

**BE-1.** So now you know how to [recap of transformation], but here's the thing. [The next problem they now have].
- Worked: "So now you know how to write a hook that stops scrolling, but here's the thing. A hook that stops scrolling won't keep them past minute three if your first body shot doesn't match." Recap plus next problem.
- Worked: "So now you know how to land your first three clients without a website, but here's the thing. Three clients won't survive if you don't have a renewal system." Sequential business pain.
- Near-miss: "So now you know how to do this, but here's the thing. There's more stuff." "More stuff" kills the bridge. Name the next problem specifically.

**BE-2.** That's the [N] [things you teach]. But here's the gap most people hit next: [specific new problem].
- Worked: "That's the four transitions every list video lives or dies on. But here's the gap most people hit next: a strong list video still needs a strong end-screen handoff or the next video doesn't get the click." Names the next problem the next video solves.
- Worked: "That's the three foundation moves. But here's the gap most people hit next: the foundation works, the offer doesn't convert, and they blame the foundation." Diagnostic gap framing.
- Near-miss: "That's the four things. But here's the gap: you still have problems." Generic gap is no gap.

**BE-3.** You now have everything you need to [primary outcome of the video]. The next problem is [the new problem the next video solves].
- Worked: "You now have everything you need to write an intro that hooks. The next problem is keeping the body of the video tight enough that the hook's promise survives to the end." Bridges video N's payoff to video N+1's problem.
- Worked: "You now have everything you need to plan a list video viewers binge. The next problem is making the thumbnail and title earn the click in the first place." Promise plus hand-off.
- Near-miss: "You now have everything you need. The next problem is more stuff." Empty bridge.

**BE-4.** [Strong recap line]. The reason most creators still don't get the result is [the new lever]. That's what I'm going to show you next.
- Worked: "Transitions are the lifeline of every multi-point video. The reason most creators still don't get the retention is the ending. That's what I'm going to show you next." Diagnoses why the recap alone isn't enough.
- Worked: "Pricing by outcome instead of hour is half the move. The reason most coaches still grind is the delivery model. That's what I'm going to show you next." Names the second lever.
- Near-miss: "That's the recap. The reason it doesn't work is something else. Watch the next video." Generic something-else equals no bridge.

**BE-5.** Look, [specific tool / pattern / system you taught] is real. But it only works if [the dependency you teach in the next video]. Watch this next.
- Worked: "Look, the 5-type hook system is real. But it only works if the body of the video pays off the hook's promise. Watch this next." Names the dependency.
- Worked: "Look, batching content is real. But it only works if you batch ideas first, not just recordings. Watch this next." Inverts the obvious application order.
- Near-miss: "Look, this is real. But there's more. Watch this next." Pure abstraction.

**BE-6.** Here's the thing nobody warns you about [the topic you just taught]: [the second-order problem]. I'm going to show you exactly how to handle it next.
- Worked: "Here's the thing nobody warns you about strong intros: a strong intro raises retention so much that your weak middle gets exposed. I'm going to show you exactly how to handle it next." Names the second-order trap.
- Worked: "Here's the thing nobody warns you about getting your first three clients: the bottleneck moves to delivery the day client three signs. I'm going to show you exactly how to handle it next." Bottleneck-shift framing.
- Near-miss: "Here's the thing nobody warns you about: stuff happens later. Watch this next." "Stuff happens later" is the empty-noun stack.

**BE-7.** That's how you [recap line in plain language]. The next thing on your stack is [specific named next problem]. I made a video on it. Watch it next.
- Worked: "That's how you write a hook that stops scrolling. The next thing on your stack is keeping retention through the middle. I made a video on it. Watch it next." Stack-language reads like a checklist viewer is on.
- Worked: "That's how you decide which format to use for which video. The next thing on your stack is writing the body of a deep dive without losing the audience. I made a video on it. Watch it next." Process-flow handoff.
- Near-miss: "That's how you do that. The next thing is the next thing. Watch it." Both halves dead.

**BE-8.** Quick recap: [one phrase]. Now the question becomes, [the next-video question]? Here's where to find the answer.
- Worked: "Quick recap: every transition forward-hooks or it dies. Now the question becomes, what does a great ending look like? Here's where to find the answer." Question-shape bridge into the next video.
- Worked: "Quick recap: package the offer around the outcome, not the deliverable. Now the question becomes, what do you charge for the outcome? Here's where to find the answer." Pricing follow-up.
- Near-miss: "Quick recap: lots of stuff. Now the question is what's next? Here's where to find it." Empty recap, empty question. Both halves cliff-fall.

## Section 4. Banned phrases

These phrases read as AI defaults or filler. They burn attention without adding anything. Replacement patterns are above.

The list is split into two tiers:

- **Tier 1 (auto-reject):** Phrases the source curriculum bans explicitly. Writing skills auto-flag and substitute. These are non-negotiable.
- **Tier 2 (soft friction):** Patterns derived from the same audio-era / AI-tell failure mode. Writing skills surface them and explain WHY, but the creator decides whether to keep. Some shows or voices use these intentionally and land them. Don't auto-reject.

### Tier 1: auto-reject (source-explicit bans)

**B-1. "Let's dive in."**
- Why it fails: AI default closer. Used so often it now signals filler. Viewers' brains tag it and disengage.
- Use instead: "All right, so let me show you..." plus the named result.

**B-2. "Let's talk about..."**
- Why it fails: "Talk about" is passive and audio-coded. Viewers come to YouTube to be SHOWN, not talked at. The verb itself promises a lecture.
- Use instead: "show you" or "walk you through" plus the named outcome.

**B-3. "Let me tell you..."**
- Why it fails: Signals an incoming list of facts the viewer has to receive. No tension, no forward hook. Same passivity problem as "talk about."
- Use instead: "Here's what's actually happening..." plus the surprising lever.

**B-6. "And finally..." / "And lastly..."**
- Why it fails: Telegraphs the end of the video, which signals "the value is over." Viewers leave instead of clicking the end-screen.
- Use instead: pivot to the next problem (BE-1 through BE-8 patterns above) without ever flagging that this is the end.

### Tier 2: soft friction (derived patterns, creator decides)

**B-4. "Without further ado..."**
- Why it tends to fail: Pure filler. The phrase exists to fill silence on stage. On video, it usually adds two seconds of nothing.
- Suggest instead: just transition. The transition itself signals movement.
- Override case: comedic delivery where the phrase is being used ironically can land.

**B-5. "Now, before we begin..."**
- Why it tends to fail: The intro is over by definition once you're transitioning. Re-opening the intro confuses orientation and tells the viewer "we're going back to setup." They tend to drop.
- Suggest instead: just transition forward.
- Override case: a deliberate disclaimer beat ("before we begin, real quick, this video assumes you've already X") can earn the reset if it raises stakes.

**B-7. "But here's where it got interesting..."**
- Why it tends to fail: AI-tell phrasing. Pattern-recognized by audiences in the last 18 months as a generative-output signature.
- Suggest instead: just deliver what got interesting. If the thing is interesting, the viewer notices without being warned.
- Override case: rare. Strong rhythmic delivery can recover it, but the safer move is to skip.

**B-8. "You won't believe what happened next."**
- Why it tends to fail: Generic curiosity bait. Same family as "this one weird trick." Viewers recognize the formula and bounce.
- Suggest instead: name the thing. Specificity beats curiosity-bait.
- Override case: rare. A documentary or true-story format with earned stakes can land it.

**B-9. "Now let me tell you a quick story."**
- Why it tends to fail: Telegraphs the move. Stories work when the viewer falls into them, not when they're announced.
- Suggest instead: just start the story. "I remember staring at the screen, watching my 50th video pull 12 views..."
- Override case: a confessional beat where the announcement IS the tonal break ("look, I'm gonna tell you a story") can work.

**B-10. "Stay tuned for..."**
- Why it tends to fail: Broadcast-era phrase. Reads as ad-break filler on streaming-native platforms.
- Suggest instead: name what's coming and why the viewer should care, not when it's coming.
- Override case: rare on YouTube; common on long-form podcast content where it's audience-native.

**B-11. "Today's video is about..."**
- Why it tends to fail: Topic-label dressed as transition. Announces a category instead of promising a payoff.
- Suggest instead: replace the topic with the result. "Today you'll learn to write a hook that stops scrolling" beats "Today's video is about hooks."
- Override case: a clear contrarian setup ("Today's video is about why everything you've been told about X is wrong") can earn the topic-frame.

**B-12. "Anyway, moving on..."**
- Why it tends to fail: Conversational stall. Reads as the speaker losing the thread. Viewer feels the show isn't planned.
- Suggest instead: a forward-hook transition (any pattern in Section 2).
- Override case: a real conversational creator (vlog tone, raw cuts) can land it as part of voice. Match the brand.

**B-13. The default verb "tell" instead of "show."**
- Why it tends to fail: "Tell" is the audio-era verb. Video viewers expect to be SHOWN. The default verb in every transition should be "show."
- Suggest instead: replace "tell" with "show" everywhere. If the thing genuinely has to be told and not shown, ask whether it belongs in this video at all.
- Override case: when the line is literally about hearing or audio ("let me tell you what she actually said"), the verb is correct.

## How skills use this file

At runtime:

1. `vid-intro` reads Section 1 plus Section 4 to generate the intro-to-body bridge (step 5 of the 6-part architecture)
2. `vid-segment` reads Section 2 plus Section 4 to generate point-to-point transitions in multi-point body sections
3. `vid-ending` reads Section 3 plus Section 4 to generate the body-to-ending bridge that earns the next-video CTA
4. Each skill loads `voice-profile.md` to filter pattern types the creator avoids and weight the ones they default to
5. Each skill picks 1-2 candidate patterns, fills slots from `Content/pieces/{slug}/brain-dump.md` or `reference-block.md`, and surfaces options
6. Banned phrases (Section 4) are auto-rejected at candidate-generation time. The creator never sees them as options.

Same anti-fabrication discipline as [[intro-architecture]]: every result, number, and receipt in a transition has to trace to the brain dump or foundation docs. Don't invent.
