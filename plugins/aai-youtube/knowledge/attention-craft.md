# Attention Craft

The mid-level retention layer. [[script-tension-architecture]] plans WHERE payoffs land across the script; [[voice-rhythm]] owns sentence-level cadence. This file owns everything between: how long a beat may live before it starts costing attention, how to wake a drifting viewer mid-beat, and how heavy a setup may be relative to its payoff.

Reference material for Claude to think with, never to paste at the creator. Examples lead, principles follow.

## 1. Pacing as felt time

A beat is not too long when you have said everything you wanted to say. It is too long the moment the viewer got it. Those are two different lines, and the gap between them is where "get to the point" comments come from.

How to hear a segment overstaying, in the read-aloud pass:

1. Mark the exact line where the point lands, the line the viewer would nod at.
2. Read every line after it. Anything that is not a re-hook is overstay.
3. The viewer tolerates a line or two of landing room. Three or more lines of explaining an already-landed point reads as stalling.

**Dead (the point landed at line 2, then kept talking):**

> "Step three is to batch your filming days. See, when you batch, what happens is you save time because you are not setting up every day, and setup is where the time goes, and you also stay in the mindset longer, so the videos come out more consistent, and consistency is what the algorithm rewards..."

Every sentence after "batch your filming days" re-explains a payoff the viewer already banked.

**Alive (payoff, then straight to the next setup):**

> "Step three is to batch your filming days. One Saturday a month, four videos done. Which creates a new problem, because batching only works if the ideas are already there, and that is step four."

The edit question is never "is this a good line?" It is "the point already landed, so what is this line FOR?" If the answer is "more of the same point," cut it. Three strong lines beat ten lines with seven passengers.

## 2. Verbal pattern interrupts

When a stretch of same-register talk runs long, the ear tunes out before the content runs out. A pattern interrupt changes the input channel for one beat and attention resets. Three writable moves:

**Cutaway.** Leave the narration for one line of concrete evidence, then come back.

> "Listen to this." [plays the cut with no smoothing, jumpy] "Now this." [plays the same cut with a crossfade] "Same cuts. Different feel."

**Direct-address shift.** Turn from the material to the viewer, mid-point.

> "Stop. Look at your last three videos. I bet two of them are the same idea wearing different hats."

**Self-interruption.** Cut your own sentence off and restart on the real point.

> "The trick is, well, no. Let me just show you."

It signals something unscripted is happening, and unscripted reads as worth watching.

The rule: one interrupt per drift point. An interrupt every three lines is not craft, it is caffeine. If every beat seems to need one, the beats are too long. Fix pacing first.

## 3. Mid-segment re-engagement

The re-hook is not only for segment boundaries. Inside a long segment, every payoff spends attention and the next line has to earn it back. Three moves, in order of strength:

1. **The curiosity-spiking payoff.** Write the payoff itself so it opens the next loop: name something the viewer now wants explained. "The third thing they all do is something I call the two-word test." The viewer banked the answer AND carries a new question in the same line. Strongest because the re-hook costs zero extra words.
2. **The consequence tease.** The payoff creates the next problem. "So: batch your filming days. Which creates a new problem, because batching only works if..."
3. **The raised-stakes callback.** Reach back to an earlier plant and raise its stakes. "Remember the five hours a week onboarding was eating? This is where they went."

If none of the three fits, the segment is probably done. End it early and hand off. A short segment that re-hooks beats a full-length one that coasts.

## 4. Plant vs payoff weighting

Match the weight of the setup to the weight of what it pays off.

- **A one-line plant earns a one-line payoff.** "Step 4 is the crossfade. Drag the right edge of the previous clip 0.3 seconds into the next. Done." The viewer asked a small question, got a complete answer, feels smart.
- **A 60-second plant promises a payoff worth the wait.** Spend a minute building the mystery and the reveal has to change something the viewer believes. A big build-up to a small answer is the cliffhanger-as-content failure in [[script-tension-architecture]].
- **The inverted miss: a thin plant on a heavy payoff.** If the payoff is the video's big moment, a one-line setup undersells it. Build the want before you spend the answer.

**The deliberate draw-out.** When the payoff IS the big moment, you may show the evidence before naming what it is, and let the viewer ask the question for you: play the clip, show the screenshot, then hold one more beat while you set up what they are looking at. "I am not going to tell you what this is yet. Watch it first." Only when the material earns the delay: the reveal has to reframe something. Drawing out a small payoff reads as teasing.

## 5. Energy direction

A segment should exit higher than it entered. Energy rises toward the payoff; the re-hook catches it before it dips. In the read-aloud pass, if the segment feels like it descends into its own ending, the last two beats are in the wrong order: the takeaway landed last and the forward hook died in front of it. Swap them so the segment exits on the rise.

**Descending (takeaway last, energy spent):**

> "...which is exactly what the next point fixes. So that is why the crossfade matters."

**Rising (hook last, energy carried forward):**

> "...so that is one line of work for audio that sounds like a person talking. Step 5 is the export setting that makes or breaks YouTube quality, and it is one checkbox."

Fast formats (news, short process) run flatter by design. Energy direction still applies inside each beat: end every beat on the thing that pulls into the next one, never on the thing that closes.

## What this file does not do

- Cross-segment tension (title promise, threads, handoffs) lives in [[script-tension-architecture]].
- Sentence cadence and paragraph shape live in [[voice-rhythm]].
- It does not script the creator's delivery. These are writing moves, not performance notes.
- It never invents a moment to interrupt with. A cutaway or callback needs real material from the brain dump or the banks. No material, no interrupt. Fix the pacing instead.
