# Metaphor Builder

A strong metaphor turns an abstract concept into something anyone can picture. Facts alone retain around 10% of viewer attention. A strong visual metaphor can push retention on that concept to 65% or higher. Most creators skip metaphors because they're hard, which is exactly why a good one is a differentiator.

This guide teaches how metaphors work. vid-bank's Stage M uses it to extract, build, and classify metaphors into the bank. Writing skills read the bank entries it produces; how a metaphor gets spliced into a script lives in [[metaphor-integration]].

## Real examples (read these first)

### Example 1: Jelly Bean Channels (visual metaphor)

**Concept being clarified:** niche channels out-earn broad channels even when the broad channel has more subscribers and watch time.

**Spoken:** "Here's two different channels. Channel One spent five years building theirs, they can afford to go full time. Channel Two spent a year, not very big, they're already paying an editor. Here's the crazy part, in a year Channel Two will be making ten times more than Channel One."

**Shown:** Two small glasses on a table. Fill one with a large handful of jelly beans, the other with just a few. Label the large glass "1" and the small one "2." Overlays appear: "$15K" above glass 1, "$150K" above glass 2.

**When it lands:** when the viewer is choosing between broad and niche and needs to see that subscriber count is a vanity metric.

**Visual:** true. The prop carries the punch. Pure speech loses most of the impact.

**Everyday category:** food (jelly beans).

### Example 2: Wine Tasting Dating (non-visual metaphor)

**Concept being clarified:** treating a first date like a guarantee sets you up to be crushed when it doesn't lead to a second one.

**The metaphor:** "Early dating is like wine tasting. You sip, you swirl, you smell. You don't chug the bottle and assume it's perfect for life. You're collecting clues before committing."

**When it lands:** when the viewer is over-invested in single interactions.

**Visual:** false. The language does the work. A prop would add nothing.

**Everyday category:** food (wine tasting).

## Metaphor vs. visual demo vs. framework visualization

Three different tools. Easy to confuse. Here's the line:

- **Metaphor** swaps a complex idea for a vivid, familiar image. "Early dating is like wine tasting." The viewer pictures the familiar thing and maps it to the concept. Can be visual (prop helps) or non-visual (speech alone).

- **Visual demo** shows the actual thing on screen. Sixteen sugar cubes next to a Coke can. The cubes ARE the sugar in the soda. There's no swap, just direct visibility. Separate tool for a separate job.

- **Framework visualization** makes an organizing structure visible. Sticky notes on a table representing scattered topics, grouped into three blocks. The sticky notes stand in for the topics. Belongs to the logic section of a video, not the metaphor slot.

If in doubt: am I comparing this concept to a different thing (metaphor), showing the thing itself (visual demo), or organizing the thing into structure (framework visualization)?

## Visual vs. non-visual metaphors

**Non-visual metaphor** lands in pure speech. "Early dating is like wine tasting" doesn't need anything on screen. The creator can deliver it talking-head style.

**Visual metaphor** needs a prop or graphic to land. Two glasses of jelly beans representing two channels falls flat if you describe it without showing it.

Both use the same 3-step builder. Both follow the same principles. The difference is delivery at script time. The classification is captured on the bank entry so downstream writing skills know whether to plan a visual or just script the speech.

**Two layers for visual metaphors.** Visual metaphors have a speech layer and a visual layer running in parallel on screen. Bank entries capture them as distinct sections: what the creator says, and what appears on camera. Someone reading the entry later (or a writing skill splicing it into a script) needs both to reproduce the moment.

How to tell visual vs. non-visual: read the metaphor aloud with no visual aid. If the viewer still gets it, it's non-visual. If the viewer needs to see something to follow, it's visual.

## The 3-step builder

Every metaphor in the bank follows this shape. Works whether the creator already has the metaphor or needs help making one.

### Step 1: Define the problem

What's the confusing, abstract, or dry idea you're trying to simplify? State it as the viewer would feel it.

Example: "People treat a first date like a guarantee and get crushed when it doesn't lead to a second one."

### Step 2: Define the solution

What do you want the viewer to do instead? Keep it to one clear move.

Example: "Slow down. Collect clues before committing."

### Step 3: Find a relatable comparison

Something everyday and familiar that works the same way. The comparison has to be something anyone would recognize.

Example: "Early dating is like wine tasting. You sip, swirl, smell. You don't chug the bottle."

If the metaphor is visual, capture the shown layer too: what prop, action, or graphic carries it.

## Everyday categories for finding comparisons

When the creator is stuck, pull from these. Anyone recognizes them.

### Food

Cooking, restaurant ordering, wine tasting, cereal aisle choices, hot vs. cold foods. Strong because everyone eats and has opinions about it.

### Cars

Test-driving, highway vs. back roads, manual vs. automatic, gas vs. electric. Strong for "journey" or "choice" concepts.

### Clothes

Trying on outfits, dress codes, tailoring, the difference between off-the-rack and bespoke. Strong for "fit" or "custom" concepts.

### Sports

Training plans, game vs. practice, team vs. solo, rookie vs. veteran. Strong for "skill acquisition" concepts.

### Travel

Directions, maps, GPS rerouting, booking flights, packing a bag. Strong for "path" or "plan" concepts.

Extend beyond these when the right comparison lives somewhere else. These are defaults, not a cage.

## Pivot phrases (the bridge back to logic)

The metaphor lands. Then the script returns to the actual teaching. Three proven bridges:

- "So what does that actually mean for you?"
- "Let me show you how that applies in real life."
- "Here's how to fix that, step by step."

These belong in the metaphor's `> [!tip] The pivot phrase` callout so the writer knows how to transition out of the metaphor cleanly.

## When NOT to use a metaphor

- The idea is already clear on its own. Don't dress up simple ideas.
- You're forcing one just to sound clever. If the first attempt doesn't click, skip.
- It's surreal or overly complex. If it feels like a riddle instead of clarity, skip.
- You've already used another metaphor at the same point. Don't stack.

Rule of thumb: if it takes 20 minutes to build and still feels awkward, drop it and use a story or a demo instead.

## Common mistakes

- **Mixing metaphors.** "Your video is a gift, and it's also a rocket ship." Pick one.
- **Over-explaining.** "So just to clarify, the box is the thumbnail because..." If you have to explain, the metaphor didn't work. Try a different comparison.
- **Dragging it out.** Metaphors should land fast. Three sentences, then pivot.
- **Weird for the sake of weird.** "It's like a dolphin juggling kumquats." Keep it familiar.
- **Conflating visual demo with metaphor.** If you're just showing the thing itself, that's a visual demo, not a metaphor. Metaphors swap one concept for a different familiar one.

## Dig deeper probes (use when the first attempt is weak)

When the creator offers an abstract metaphor:
- "Make it something anyone would recognize. What's the everyday version?"
- "What do you see in your kitchen that works the same way?"

When the metaphor feels forced:
- "Is there a simpler comparison that gets at the same thing?"
- "Would it work better to drop the metaphor and just tell a story here?"

When the creator isn't sure it lands:
- "Read it back. If someone heard this at a coffee shop, would they nod or ask a question?"

For visual metaphors specifically:
- "Describe what's on screen. If you can't picture it clearly, the shown layer needs more detail."
- "Could someone else recreate the visual from what you just described?"

## Source note

Metaphors are remixed by nature. Whether the creator invented a metaphor or heard it from someone else doesn't matter for the bank. Capture either way. If the creator wants to remember where they first heard it, that goes in the Notes section as free text. No separate schema for origin.
