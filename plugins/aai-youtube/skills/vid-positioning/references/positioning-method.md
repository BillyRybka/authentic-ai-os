# Positioning Method

The method `vid-positioning` uses to draft the Iceberg Statement. Read this when the SKILL.md tells you to. Don't paste it into chat.

## The four parts (all required)

A sharp Iceberg Statement has four components. If a draft is missing one, it's not sharp yet.

- **WHO.** The avatar, in their words. Pulled from the Avatar section of `foundation/avatar.md`. Don't try to renarrow the avatar here. If the avatar is broad, the specificity moves into TENSION and HOW.
- **WHAT.** The result the avatar walks away with. Concrete, not a category. "Revenue-generating content" not "content." "Thirty pounds lighter" not "healthier." "First hire in place" not "team growth."
- **HOW.** The mechanism. What the method actually does. The differentiating move. "Turn their expertise into," "automate the inbox," "spot red flags before the first date." This is what separates this creator from every other one in the niche.
- **TENSION.** An enemy, refused axis, or specific stakes that makes the line bite. Without it, the statement reads like 1000 other channels.

## The classic shape

> "I help [WHO] [HOW] to get [WHAT] without [TENSION]."

The connectors flex. "Without" can become "before," "instead of," "the way that," or drop entirely. The four components have to be present in some form.

### A worked example

> "I help new managers lead high-performing teams without becoming micromanagers."

- WHO: new managers
- HOW: (implicit method, learned across the channel)
- WHAT: high-performing teams
- TENSION: named enemy ("becoming micromanagers")

### Another worked example

> "I help busy parents feed their family healthy weeknight meals without spending Sunday meal prepping."

- WHO: busy parents
- HOW: (implicit method, learned across the channel) feed their family
- WHAT: healthy weeknight meals
- TENSION: spending Sunday meal prepping

## Three places the tension can live

Pick the one the creator gave you loudest.

### 1. Named enemy

What does the avatar refuse to become, create, sound like, or be associated with? Bake it in.

- "...without becoming the micromanager everyone avoids."
- "...without giving up pizza."
- "...without the cry it out method."

If the creator named it in 1 to 3 words, especially if they said it twice, use those literal words. See the literal-words rule below.

### 2. Refused axis

What's the niche default everyone competes on? Don't compete there. Find the axis being ignored and own it.

- Volvo refused to compete on horsepower. Owned safety.
- Red Bull refused to compete on taste. Owned energy.
- A finance creator refused to compete on tips and shortcuts. Owned a system.

In the Iceberg Statement, the refused axis usually shows up as what the avatar gets that nobody else delivers: "...using a system" (refusing tips), "...the way their audience never loses trust" (refusing speed-and-scale).

### 3. Specific stakes or moment

What's the worst moment if this stays broken? Or the moment the win lands? Bake the moment in.

- "...before they ever walk into an open house."
- "...before their kid graduates."
- "...before their first hire quits."

Specific stakes work when the creator's positioning lives in a moment of urgency the avatar recognizes.

## The literal-words rule for TENSION

If the creator named the enemy in 1 to 3 words, especially if they said it more than once unprompted, use those exact words. Don't paraphrase.

- "Cry it out" stays "cry it out." Not "extinction-based sleep training" or "letting them self-soothe."
- "Fake gurus" stays "fake gurus." Not "people pretending to be experts."
- "Hourly-rate model" stays "hourly-rate model." Not "trading time for money."
- "Cold outreach" stays "cold outreach." Not "spam-style prospecting."

Why this matters: a phrase the creator already says becomes a brand asset. Every video reinforces it. A paraphrase loses that. The creator will read a paraphrased line aloud and reword it back to the original.

Signals the creator handed you a literal-words enemy:

- They said it twice or more in their answers.
- They said it without prompting.
- They said it with conviction (the cadence changed when they hit the phrase).
- They used it as a category, not a description (e.g. "cry it out" as a category of approach, not "the baby cried it out" as a description).

If you see those signals, lock the phrase verbatim. Don't ask permission to use it. Just put it in the draft.

## Drafting protocol

After WHO, WHAT, HOW, and TENSION are clear:

1. Draft 2 candidate statements. Both keep the same WHO, WHAT, and HOW. The TENSION varies.
2. Show both. Tell the creator plainly: "Two drafts. Which is closer, and what's wrong with it?"
3. The creator reacts. Rewrite using whatever they said. Show the new version.
4. Keep going until they read it aloud without rewording.

Don't show three rewordings of the same template. The two drafts should differ in TENSION (different enemy, or one enemy and one refused-axis), not in phrasing.

## When a draft is bland

Three checks. If a draft fails one, swap the TENSION or sharpen the WHAT.

### Check 1: Could 1000 other channels in the niche say this exact sentence?

If yes, the TENSION isn't strong enough or the WHAT is too generic. Find a sharper enemy. Tighten the WHAT from category to concrete result.

Bland: "I help managers become better leaders."
Sharper: "I help new managers lead high-performing teams without becoming micromanagers."

### Check 2: Can the viewer picture themselves searching this on YouTube?

If no, the WHAT is too abstract. Replace abstract nouns with concrete moments.

Bland: "I help homebuyers understand the buying process."
Sharper: "I help first-time buyers make confident home decisions before they ever walk into an open house."

### Check 3: Does the creator read it aloud without rewording?

If they reword anything, the reworded version IS the statement. Their natural cadence is the test. Don't argue with the read-aloud result.

## Reading examples for calibration

Open `positioning-examples.md`. Find the niche closest to the creator's avatar. Read one or two examples, not all of them.

Why one or two: reading all 10 trains you to pattern-match the shape of an example. Pattern-matching kills sharpness because every creator's actual language is different. The examples are calibration for what "sharp" feels like, not templates to match.

If the creator's language gives you a structurally different shape than any example, use the creator's shape. The examples don't override the creator.

## What "sharp" feels like (the calibration signal)

A sharp statement triggers one of these reactions when you read it back:

- The creator's eyes light up.
- The creator says "yeah, that's exactly it" without hesitation.
- The creator immediately starts thinking about which video to make first.
- The creator rewords it slightly in a way that's even sharper.

A bland statement triggers one of these:

- The creator says "yeah, that works."
- The creator pauses and says "I mean, kinda."
- The creator asks "do I have to say it that way?"
- The creator gives a polite "let's go with that, I guess."

If you see bland signals, the statement isn't done. Run the bland-check, swap the TENSION, and try again.

## Anti-patterns

- Drafting before WHO, WHAT, HOW, and TENSION are visible in the inputs. Read first. Draft second.
- Asking the creator to draft the statement. Claude drafts. Creator reacts.
- Showing three rewordings of one template. The two drafts must differ in TENSION.
- Paraphrasing a literal enemy phrase the creator named. Lock it verbatim.
- Pattern-matching the shape of an example. Use the creator's actual language.
- Locking the statement when the creator gives polite acceptance instead of "that's it." Polite acceptance is a bland signal.
- Skipping the read-aloud test.
- Trying to renarrow the avatar inside this skill. That was `vid-avatar`'s job. If the avatar is broad, the specificity moves into TENSION and HOW.
