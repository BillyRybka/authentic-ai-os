# Positioning Examples

> **STOP. Find ONE niche, read ONE example, close the file.** Reading all 10 trains pattern-matching. Pattern-matching kills sharpness. The examples are calibration for what "sharp" feels like, not templates to copy.

Locked Iceberg Statement examples for calibration. Read one or two closest to the creator's niche when drafting. Don't read all of them. Don't pattern-match the shape.

The examples below break out the four components (WHO, WHAT, HOW, TENSION) so you can see how each one carries tension. The shape is illustrative. Whatever the creator's actual language gives you wins.

## Examples by niche

### Relationships / recovery

> "I help women spot, survive, and heal from narcissistic relationships."

- WHO: women
- WHAT: spot, survive, heal from narcissistic relationships
- HOW: (implicit across the channel)
- TENSION: named enemy ("narcissistic relationships")

What works: the WHAT is a three-step verb arc that mirrors the viewer's actual journey. The enemy is specific (narcissistic, not "toxic" or "difficult").

### Sleep coaching / new parents

> "I help new parents get their baby sleeping through the night by 12 weeks without the cry it out method."

- WHO: new parents
- WHAT: baby sleeping through the night
- HOW: a structured 12-week protocol
- TENSION: named enemy ("cry it out method")

What works: WHAT has a specific timeframe (12 weeks). HOW is named (a protocol, not just "tips"). TENSION is the niche default the creator refuses, in the creator's own language.

### Fitness / busy parents

> "I help busy dads get strong and lean using short 20-minute workouts with no gym required."

- WHO: busy dads
- WHAT: strong and lean
- HOW: short 20-minute workouts
- TENSION: refused axis ("no gym required" refuses the standard fitness niche default)

What works: WHO carries a life constraint (busy, dads). The HOW + TENSION answers the niche's #1 objection upfront.

### Fitness / weight loss

- WHO: busy dads
- WHAT: lose 30 pounds
- HOW: (implicit method across the channel)
- TENSION: named enemy ("giving up pizza" refuses restrictive dieting)

What works: WHAT has a specific number. TENSION names the niche default (giving up the foods you like) and refuses it.

### Dating / men's

## Failed positioning statements (and why)

These are real shapes of weak statements. Use them as a sniff test against any draft.

- *"I help people gain more confidence."* WHO is everyone alive. WHAT is invisible. No HOW. No TENSION. Could be dating, public speaking, body image, business.
- *"I help entrepreneurs grow their online presence."* "Entrepreneurs" spans tech founders to Etsy sellers. "Online presence" is the most generic agency phrase. No HOW. No TENSION.
- *"I help people who want to be healthy feel their best."* Two unobservables stacked.
- *"I help busy men get fit."* "Busy men" is 80% of adult men. No HOW. No TENSION.
- *"I help service providers grow their business."* "Service providers" spans plumbers to consultants. "Grow their business" is a non-promise.
- *"I help adults lose weight and get healthy."* Niche default. No constraint, method, number, or named enemy.
- *"I help small business owners with operational inefficiency."* MBA-textbook framing. Viewers don't search "operational inefficiency."
- *"I help creators succeed on YouTube."* "Creators" plus "succeed" plus "YouTube" are all words 10,000 channels use. No axis owned.
- *"I help women improve their confidence and attract better partners."* Two WHATs stitched together means the creator hasn't picked one.
- *"I help people replace digital with analogue solutions."* No WHO. No HOW. The audience came for the niche the creator was in (a notebook video), not the philosophy. Got 9,300 views on the next video.

## The Volvo and Red Bull lessons

Positioning is built through repetition on ONE axis, not by competing on many.

**Volvo:** WHO = families with young kids. WHAT = a safe car. TENSION = refused axis. Volvo never competes on horsepower. If they did, they'd be against every car company in the world.

**Red Bull:** WHO = millennials. WHAT = energy. TENSION = refused axis. Red Bull never competes on taste. Red Bull tastes like Volvo fuel. Competing on taste would put them against 99% of all drinks.

The lesson: find the axis your competition ignores and own it.

## The "known for one thing" principle

Goal: become associated with a single word or concept in the viewer's mind. Red Bull = Energy. Volvo = Safety.

Niche creators usually become "the X guy" or "the X lady": the ads guy, the narcissist lady, the cheese lady. Being known for ONE thing is the mechanism of growth.

The Iceberg Statement names the ONE thing.

## Case study: the notebook creator

One creator posted a video titled "This pocket notebook is (slowly) replacing my phone." It did 3.9M views.

- Next video: 9,300 views.
- Next video: 2,800 views.
- Later, back on notebooks: 167,000 views.

The viral video's audience came for the notebook plus the WHO plus the WHAT. The next videos had different topics. The audience didn't come back. When the creator returned to the same combination, the audience did.

A viral video without a clear Iceberg Statement does NOT build a channel.

## If the creator is a professional who never had the problem

Doctors, physiotherapists, consultants who teach something they didn't personally struggle with. The Iceberg Statement still works. The WHO is the avatar, not the creator. The WHAT and HOW come from the creator's professional experience. Backstory (built by `vid-backstory`) handles the "did the creator have this problem" question separately.

Never fabricate. If the creator has client cases, the statement can lean on those for proof. If they don't yet, flag the MVP caveat: the statement is the working hypothesis and refines once real videos publish.

## Anti-patterns

- Competing on a crowded differentiator. If everyone in the niche says "faster," don't also say "faster."
- Generic WHO ("anyone who wants to be healthy").
- Vague WHAT ("be happier").
- Chasing viral hits without locking the Iceberg Statement first. Builds subscribers who won't return.
- Changing the Iceberg Statement every few months. Never builds association.
- Stitching two WHATs together ("improve confidence AND attract partners"). Pick one.
