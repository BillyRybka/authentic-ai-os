# Emotion Brick Decision Matrix

Examples-first. Five questions decide which brick a segment uses. Each brick maps to a sub-type. Each sub-type has a worked example and a near-miss so the boundary is clear.

This file is reference material for Claude to think with at structure-pass time. The creator never sees the matrix; the skill picks the brick, surfaces it as part of the structure draft, and lets the creator override.

## The matrix

Ask in order. The first YES answer wins.

1. **Is the problem invisible or hard for the viewer to see / feel?** YES → Visual Demo: Show-the-Problem
2. **Will a clear before-and-after silence doubt about why the old way doesn't work?** YES → Visual Demo: Contrast
3. **Is there a real-world example or thumbnail / page / clip that can be paused, zoomed, or annotated to make the point?** YES → Visual Demo: Breakdown
4. **Does the viewer need to FEEL the pain or urgency of staying stuck?** YES → Story
5. **Is the idea abstract, hard to grasp, or too familiar to feel fresh?** YES → Metaphor

If multiple YES, surface ALL matched bricks as candidates. The three brick types are peers in the source teaching, not a ranked hierarchy. Source treats Visual Demo, Story, and Metaphor as separate tools with separate when-to-use criteria (lessons 7, 8, 9 respectively). Lesson 9 specifically notes metaphors drive some of the strongest performing creator content. Pick by fit to THIS segment's idea and the creator's voice, not by a standing rank.

A few non-binding fit cues that may help the creator pick when multiple bricks match:

- Visual demos land fastest when the viewer's eye can do the cognitive work (the problem is genuinely invisible, or a contrast is sharper than a description).
- Stories carry farther when the viewer needs to FEEL the consequence and the creator has a real lived moment to anchor.
- Metaphors compress hardest when the idea is abstract, familiar, or both — and the comparison is one the avatar already knows.

Surface 1-3 candidate bricks (one per matched YES) so the creator can pick. Don't auto-default to Visual Demo just because it scored first.

If all NO: the segment may be a tight Logic-only step (short process default) or the brick is supplied by a prior segment. Confirm with the format planner before writing.

## Brick 1: Visual Demo, Show-the-Problem

Turns invisible into visible. Numbers become piles. Hidden mistakes become slow-mo. Use when stating the number alone would make viewers shrug.

**Worked (food / health niche):**

Concept: soda is why the viewer isn't losing weight.

Spoken: "Would you lose weight if you ate this much sugar a day? That's how much is in one Coke. With your average daily intake, you're closer to 26 sugar cubes a day."

Shown: 16 sugar cubes stacked next to a Coke can on a kitchen counter.

Why it lands: invisible (sugar dissolved in liquid) becomes visible (pile of cubes). The viewer's brain does the math without doing math. Emotional reaction (shock at the pile) primes them for the logic that follows.

**Worked (creator-economy niche):**

Concept: the gap between a creator's first 10 videos and their 20th video is invisible until you see it stacked.

Spoken: "Most creators give up between video 10 and 15 because growth feels nonexistent. Here's what 10 versus 20 actually looks like."

Shown: two horizontal bar charts on screen. Left bar (videos 1-10) shows tiny gold blips on a sea of red dots. Right bar (videos 11-20) shows half gold, half red. Caption: "10 of 20" not "1 of 10."

Why it lands: turns the abstract "growth is non-linear" into a chart anyone can read in 2 seconds.

**Near-miss (Show-the-Problem with too many elements):**

Same concept, but the chart has 5 colors representing different metrics, three captions, and a small-text legend.

Why it misses: complexity kills visual demos. The 5-color chart fails because the viewer can't extract the point in 2 seconds. Two colors works. Three is the warning line. Four+ is noise.

**Near-miss (Show-the-Problem when the problem is already visible):**

Concept: most YouTubers post inconsistent thumbnails.

Spoken: "Most YouTubers post inconsistent thumbnails."

Shown: a grid of 12 thumbnails labeled "inconsistent."

Why it misses: the problem (inconsistent thumbnails) is already visible. The grid IS the problem, but the viewer already saw it. Show-the-Problem turns INVISIBLE into visible. Use Contrast Demo instead (consistent grid vs. inconsistent grid).

## Brick 2: Visual Demo, Contrast

Side-by-side: old way vs. new way, wrong vs. right, before vs. after. Triggers the "Version A vs. Version B" moment. Viewer realizes they're version A, wants to be version B.

**Worked (sales-page review):**

Concept: feature-led headlines kill conversion vs. transformation-led headlines.

Spoken: "Same product. Same price. Same target customer. Two versions."

Shown: Left half of screen, original sales page with headline "Final Cut Pro Color Grading Masterclass" and a screenshot of color adjustment rings. Right half, same page, headline "Stick out faster on social media with HBO-quality video," replaced with a before/after split of dull footage versus cinematic footage.

Why it lands: same offer, two pages, viewer sees the transformation. The viewer who's been writing feature-led headlines now sees themselves in the left side and wants the right side.

**Worked (productivity niche):**

Concept: morning routines that protect deep work vs. morning routines that destroy it.

Spoken: "Same person. Same job. Two mornings."

Shown: Split-screen video. Left: phone-checking, slack-replying, breakfast-doomscrolling, first deep-work block at 10:30am. Right: phone-in-other-room, walk, breakfast, first deep-work block at 7:45am. Both labeled with timestamps.

Why it lands: the same person is the constant; the routine is the variable. Visual proof that the morning IS the lever.

**Near-miss (Contrast with 5 contrasts):**

Same morning routine concept, but the split-screen tracks 8 different metrics across 5 different days.

Why it misses: contrast demos work with 1-3 contrasts. More than 3 and the viewer can't hold the comparison in working memory. Pick the strongest 1-3 and cut the rest.

**Near-miss (Contrast where the "old way" isn't actually wrong):**

Concept: handwritten morning pages vs. typed morning pages.

Spoken: "Old way is handwritten. New way is typed. Look at the difference."

Why it misses: handwriting vs. typing isn't a wrong/right contrast, it's a preference. Contrast Demo needs one side to be CLEARLY worse. If the contrast is actually a tradeoff, drop the brick or use Breakdown Demo to teach when to use which.

## Brick 3: Visual Demo, Breakdown

Pause, zoom, annotate a real thing on screen. Best for visually specific skills (design, marketing, body language, analytics, sales pages, thumbnails).

**Worked (Airbnb listing breakdown):**

Concept: what makes an Airbnb listing get booked vs. ignored.

Spoken: "Look at this listing. Here's why it books at 87% occupancy."

Shown: full screen of the listing page, then arrows zooming to:
1. The main photo: "hooks the second the page loads"
2. The title: "'2 minutes from town', immediate benefit, not a feature"
3. The description bullets: "WiFi, easy check-in, walkable, only what bookers care about"

Why it lands: the listing is on screen. The viewer follows the arrows. Each annotation makes one invisible thing visible (why this photo, why this title, why these bullets). The cognitive work is done by the screen, not the viewer.

**Worked (charisma breakdown):**

Concept: why this talk-show host's openings always land.

Spoken: "Watch how Craig Ferguson opens this conversation."

Shown: 6-second clip of the opening. Then freeze frame with annotation: "1. STARTS ALMOST EVERY CONVERSATION IN A FUN POSITIVE WAY." Then play the next 4 seconds. Freeze. Annotation: "2. ASKS A SPECIFIC QUESTION ABOUT A DETAIL OF THEIR LIFE."

Why it lands: the invisible (charisma) becomes visible (two specific moves you can name). Anyone could rewatch the clip and see the moves now.

**Near-miss (Breakdown where the screen is too cluttered):**

Same listing concept, but the screenshot has the booking widget, the calendar, the reviews, the pricing, the host's other listings, all visible at once. Arrows fly all over the screen.

Why it misses: viewers can't process where to look. Breakdown Demos need the screenshot CROPPED to isolate the single element being taught. Crop, then annotate.

## Brick 4: Story

The viewer needs to FEEL the pain (or the win) before the lesson lands. Problem-Action-Outcome format, 20-30 seconds spoken aloud. See `knowledge/story-capture-guide.md` for the canonical structure and the 6 prompts.

**Worked (Pricing story, own):**

Problem: "When I launched my first course I priced it at $49 because I was scared to charge more."

Action: "I worked at that price for three months."

Outcome: "I made $900, and realized I'd built myself a minimum wage job."

Why it lands: specific number ($49), specific timeline (three months), specific result ($900), and a twist that becomes the lesson (minimum wage job). The viewer feels the trap.

**Worked (Client story):**

Problem: "My client Steve was running ads to a sales page converting at 0.7% and was about to shut the whole funnel down."

Action: "We changed one thing. The headline went from a feature label to a transformation promise."

Outcome: "Conversion jumped to 4.2% the same week. Same traffic, same offer."

Why it lands: client name (Steve, credible), specific before number (0.7%), specific after number (4.2%), specific timeline (same week), specific change (headline only, the lesson is in the change).

**Near-miss (Story that starts at the journey's beginning):**

"I started my business in 2018. For a long time things were okay. Then around 2021 things got harder. By 2023 I figured out something important..."

Why it misses: viewers drop. Stories start at the WORST MOMENT of the problem, not the beginning of the journey. Start at the moment of struggle.

**Near-miss (Story without specifics):**

"My client was struggling. We did something different. It worked great."

Why it misses: no name, no number, no specific action, no specific result. Generic stories activate zero emotional pathways. If the bank entry is this thin, route to `vid-capture` to dig deeper before pulling.

## Brick 5: Metaphor

The idea is abstract or too familiar. A familiar comparison makes it click. See `knowledge/metaphor-builder.md` for the 3-step builder. See `knowledge/metaphor-integration.md` for placement and the bridge-back-to-logic.

**Worked (channel-strategy niche):**

Concept: niche channels out-earn broad channels even when subscriber count is smaller.

Metaphor: "Two glasses on a table. One full of jelly beans, one with just a few. The big glass is your subscriber count. The small glass is your revenue. Niche channels are pouring beans into the small glass."

Why it lands: visual + everyday (jelly beans, glasses). Familiar object. Concept (subscriber count vs. revenue) becomes spatially obvious.

**Worked (content-system niche):**

Concept: most creators create content from a blank page every week.

Metaphor: "Most creators are running a restaurant with no recipes. Every Monday someone walks in and says 'what's good today?' and the chef has to invent the menu from scratch. Half the time the food's great. Half the time the chef burns out."

Why it lands: everyday image (restaurant, recipes, chef burnout) maps cleanly to the content problem. Viewer sees themselves as the chef.

**Near-miss (mixed metaphor):**

"Your business is like a garden. You plant seeds, but you're also building a rocket ship that's about to launch into orbit."

Why it misses: garden + rocket ship are two different metaphors smashed together. Pick one. The brain can't hold both.

**Near-miss (over-explained metaphor):**

"Your business is like a garden. You plant seeds, water them, weed them, fertilize them, prune the dead branches, rotate the crops, mulch the soil, and harvest at the right time. The seeds are your customers. The water is your marketing. The weeds are your competitors. The fertilizer is your messaging. The pruning is your customer cuts. The crop rotation is your offer mix. The mulching is your operations. The harvest is your revenue."

Why it misses: a metaphor lands fast or doesn't land. Three sentences max. If it takes a paragraph to explain the metaphor, the metaphor isn't working. Drop it and use a story or demo.

## Bridge-back-to-logic

Every emotion brick needs a transition into the logic block. Three proven bridges (from `knowledge/metaphor-builder.md` and the source material's transition guidance):

- "So what does that actually mean for you?"
- "Here's how to apply this in real life."
- "Here's how to fix that, step by step."

The bridge is one sentence. Don't drag it. The viewer is primed; deliver the lesson.

## What about Testimonials and Frameworks?

Testimonials and frameworks aren't emotion-brick types. They're logic-block components that get pulled IN ADDITION to the emotion brick.

- **Testimonial.** Drops in as social proof inside the logic block. "And here's what [Client] said about that exact week" + a verbatim quote in a `> [!quote]` callout.
- **Framework.** IS the logic block when the segment is teaching a creator-owned named system. The brick precedes it (story / metaphor / demo); the framework is what the brick sets up.

See `knowledge/proof-placement-rules.md` for proof and testimonial placement. See `vid-segment/references/framework-shapes.md` for which framework shape to use when (skill-local to vid-segment).

## How vid-segment uses this file

At Phase 2 (structure pass), the skill:

1. Pulls the segment's job from `piece.md` and the format planner
2. Asks the 5 matrix questions silently against the segment's job
3. Surfaces ALL matched brick types (every YES) as candidates, plus the format planner's segment shape default if relevant
4. For each candidate brick type, surfaces 0-3 bank candidates that match
5. Lets the creator pick the brick type AND swap candidates

If the brick choice and the format planner conflict (e.g. listicle Point 4 should default to a brick but the matrix says all-NO), the format planner wins. The brick exists for a structural reason in the format, even if the matrix doesn't fire.
