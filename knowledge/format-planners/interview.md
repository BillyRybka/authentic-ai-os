# Interview Format Planner

A 10-30 minute conversation with a guest who has achieved what the audience wants. Edit aggressively. Plan the package BEFORE the recording. The end-screen is critical because the guest's credibility doesn't transfer to the host automatically.

## When to use this format

- **Viewer type:** Audience interested in the guest's domain. Often listening (background) more than watching. Interviews get long watch-time partly because they're put on like a podcast.
- **Goals:** Views OR Emails. **Not Sales.** Interviews almost never convert directly. There's a documented case of a 300k-view interview producing zero sales because the credibility flowed to the guest, not the host.
- **Creator level:** Any level. Just understand the tradeoff (credibility goes to the guest, not you).
- **Best used for:** building relationships with notable guests, riding their credibility for views, teaching through someone else's transformation.
- **Format scoring:** Views 4/5, Sales 1/5, Trust 2/5. Trust scores low because the credibility transfers to the guest, not to you. See the section below; that transfer is the whole reason the end-screen routing carries this format.

### The hard truth about interviews

Interviews don't raise the host's credibility. All credibility goes to the guest. The end-screen routing is the entire monetization mechanism: route the new viewer (who arrived for the guest) to a video where YOU are the expert.

Also: guests virtually never promote the interview themselves. Don't count on it.

## The structure

```
INTRO (heavy lifting on credibility) → QUESTIONS (parable → principle per question ideally) → END
```

The intro does most of the work. The questions section is mostly the guest answering. Your job is to elicit stories first, then logic and steps.

## Intro adaptation

Interview intros are **completely different from every other format.** Almost the entire intro is loading credibility on the guest, not on the creator.

| Universal intro step | Interview default | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Top 3 viewer questions | Same. Usually variants of "who is this person?", "why should I trust them?", "what's the specific thing they did?" | Different question set than other formats. |
| 2. Hook | Often: "This is [Guest Name], and they [remarkable specific achievement]." | The hook IS the guest's identity plus their remarkable thing. Question, Statement, and Fact hooks all work. The engine is the specific achievement. |
| 3. Problem/Result | The GUEST'S problem and result, not the creator's | "She built a system to [solve specific problem]" plus "Over the past few years she's [generated specific result]." Frames the interview as a transformation case. |
| 4. Setup | 2-3 things the viewer will walk away with from the conversation | Standard setup. The things being delivered come from the guest, not from a methodology the creator owns. |
| 5. Transition | "Let me bring her in" or "Here's [Guest]." Straight into the questions | Tighter than other formats. Get to the guest. |
| 6. Credibility line | **Stacked aggressively on the GUEST.** Specific numbers, notable results, unusual proofs | "Even made a dog trainer $40k/month." Pile on. The more credibility loaded on the guest, the more viewers stay. |

**Pattern:** Hook, then guest's problem, then guest's result, then guest's credibility, then set up 2-3 things viewer will walk away with, then transition.

**Don't talk about yourself in the interview intro.** Cold viewers came for the guest. Save the creator's credibility for end-screen-linked content where they are the expert.

## The 7-step planner

1. **Pick the guest.** They must have achieved what your viewers want. Not shy. Has actual insights (verify before inviting).
2. **Set goal.** Views or Emails. Not sales.
3. **Thumbnails and title BEFORE the interview.** Pre-call the guest, find the strongest angle, lock the package. Tell the guest: "This is what we're building the interview around."
4. **Viewer's top 3 questions.** Based on title and thumbnail.
5. **Plan questions.** Every question should force the guest to start with a story (emotion), then deliver logic and steps. Example: "What was the moment you realized you needed to X, and what was stopping you from solving it?"
6. **Write intro.** Load credibility heavily, rehearse before recording. The intro gets recorded separately and added in edit.
7. **Produce and edit.** Record via Riverside or similar. **Edit aggressively.** 45 minutes down to 10 is common. Cut tangents, weak answers, anything that doesn't serve the package.

## Question planning

Every question should:
1. Force the guest to **start with a story** (emotion before logic)
2. Lead to a **specific actionable insight**
3. Tie back to one of the viewer's top 3 questions

Examples of strong question shapes:
- "What was the moment you realized X?"
- "What was the one thing stopping you from Y?"
- "Walk me through the day you decided to Z."
- "What's the one thing you wish you'd known before [achievement]?"
- "If you had to do it again from zero, what would you do differently?"

Weak question shapes:
- "Tell me about your business" (too broad, guest will ramble)
- "What's your advice for X?" (generic advice without story equals boring)
- Yes/no questions (kills momentum)

## Packaging-first approach

Lock title and thumbnail BEFORE the interview. Then build every question around matching or exceeding those expectations. Tell the guest the angle so they answer accordingly.

This breaks the conventional "interview first, package after" approach. It's why some interviews land and others sprawl.

If the guest isn't famous, **never lead with their name in the title.** Lead with their remarkable achievement. "The Man Making YouTubers Rich For Free" beats "[Guest Name] On YouTube Marketing." The achievement carries the curiosity, the name does nothing for a viewer who hasn't heard it.

## Edit aggressively

- Most of the interview gets cut. Plan for it.
- Remove any question where the answer wasn't strong.
- Remove every "um," "you know," tangent, or repeat statement.
- Top long-form interview channels routinely cut 50%+ of recorded material. Cut more than that if the answer doesn't carry weight.
- A 10-minute cut from a 45-minute interview is normal.

## Common mistakes

- Leading with the guest's name when they're not famous
- Recording without locked title and thumbnail (interview sprawls without a target)
- Pitching products in the video (sales conversion is wrong for this format)
- Letting the guest ramble. Non-media-trained guests need editing protection
- End-screening to ANOTHER interview (credibility never transfers to the host)
- Asking "tell me about your business" instead of forcing a story
- Counting on the guest to promote (they won't)

## Output format

Recorded conversation, then aggressively edited. Intro is scripted and recorded separately. Lower-thirds and visual supports added in post.

## Conversion strategy by goal

### Goal = Views (recommended)

- NO external links in description
- **End screen → a video where YOU are the expert.** This is the entire monetization mechanism. Convert new viewers (who came for the guest) into your channel audience.

### Goal = Emails

- Lead magnet related to the guest's expertise area or to your channel's broader value
- Mention 2x: opening (after credibility load) and closing
- End screen → past high-converting email video where you are the expert

### NOT for Sales

If sales is the goal, deep dive or success story is the right format. Interviews tank for sales. Don't fight it.

## Title formulas

- "The [Person Type] [Doing Remarkable Specific Thing]"
- "[Guest Name] On How They [Specific Achievement]" (only if guest is famous)
- "How [Guest] Built [Specific Result]"
- "[Specific Achievement]. [Guest Name] Reveals How"
- "The Truth About [Topic] From Someone Who [Specific Achievement]"

If the guest isn't famous, lead with the achievement, not the name. Example: "The Man Making YouTubers Rich For Free." The achievement carries the curiosity. The name comes after (or in the thumbnail's secondary line).

## Thumbnail strategies for this format

Lean into: **Social Hacking** (when guest IS famous to your audience), **Cognitive Dissonance** (when guest's achievement contradicts conventional wisdom), **Result** (when the guest's number is striking enough to carry the thumbnail alone).

Use emotional facial expressions from the guest. Make it visually obvious it's an interview (often by showing both creator and guest in frame).

## End screen routing: THE single most important post-publish move

The end screen is the entire reason interviews can grow a channel. The viewer arrived for the guest's credibility. They watched, they're warm. **Now route them to a video where YOU are the expert.** Not another interview.

If interviews always end-screen to more interviews, you become a podcast. Viewers come for guests, not for you. Channel never grows on the host's brand.

Always end-screen to evergreen content where you're the authority.

## Strategy for compounding results

- Use interviews opportunistically to ride spikes when notable guests are available
- Don't make interviews a regular cadence unless your channel's whole identity is interview-based
- The interview's real value is often what happens AFTER recording. The relationship with the guest can compound (collaborations, audience cross-pollination, joint offers)

## What to skip

- Don't lead with non-famous guest names
- Don't pitch products
- Don't end-screen to other interviews (kills the host-credibility funnel)
- Don't expect guest promotion
- Don't record before locking title and thumbnail
- Don't let the interview run long without aggressive editing
