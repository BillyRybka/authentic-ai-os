# Roast (Review) Format Planner

A 10-25 minute video reviewing 1-3 viewer submissions, showing problems and demonstrating fixes. The highest-converting format when it works. Some have generated $3 per view. Never call it a roast publicly. Call it a "free review."

## When to use this format

- **Viewer type:** Viewers who want to see if the creator can fix their specific problem. Submission-driven audience that's actively engaged.
- **Goals:** Sales (recommended) OR Emails. Views can spike. The format's strength is conversion.
- **Creator level:** Intermediate to advanced. You must be a true expert who can deliver real solutions on the spot.
- **Best used for:** establishing undeniable credibility ("watch me fix this live"), generating recurring submissions for content, building a list of submission-ready viewers who self-identify as buyers.
- **Format scoring:** Views 2/5, Sales 5/5, Trust 5/5. The low reach is the tradeoff, not a failure state. Smaller channels struggle to run this one, because it needs an audience willing to submit work.

### Why this is the highest-conversion format

Showing you can fix someone's problem is more persuasive than claiming you can. It's proof in action. Viewers self-identify as buyers when they submit (they have the problem, they want it fixed) and when they watch (they want to know if the fix would work for them too).

Channels that own this format dominate their niche.

## The structure

```
INTRO → REVIEW 1 (problem + solution) → REVIEW 2 → REVIEW 3 → END
```

Each review equals problem (parable: visual demo of what's wrong) plus solution (principle with actionable insight).

**Tone benchmark:** stern-but-kind expert at 7/10, not 10/10. Be frank. Stay kind. The fixes are the point, not the takedowns. Cruelty kills the conversion (viewers stop seeing themselves in the contestant's seat).

**Two contestant strategies:**
1. **Themed.** All 3 contestants have the same problem (e.g., all bad thumbnails). Lets you compare fix approaches across cases.
2. **Variety.** Different problems per contestant (e.g., one thumbnail, one title, one script). Broader appeal.

Test both. There's no right answer.

## Intro adaptation

Roast intros are **submission-and-stakes-driven.** The hook usually IS a contestant submission or the contrast between bad and fixed.

| Universal intro step | Roast default | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Top 3 viewer questions | Same. Usually variants of "what's getting fixed?", "can I learn from this?", "could you fix mine?" | Frames the review as something the viewer can apply to themselves. |
| 2. Hook | Often the contestant's submission OR a contrast (here's what they have, here's what's wrong, here's what we'll fix) | Statement Hook works, typically paired with a Visual Demo parable. The submission visible on screen carries the energy while the spoken hook frames the stakes. Contrarian Hook works when the contestant got something widely-believed wrong. The format gives you raw visual material. Use it as the parable, not as the hook itself. |
| 3. Problem/Result | One line: "Today we're fixing 3 [thing] and showing you exactly what's broken and what's working." | The format's promise IS clear from the intro because the visual material carries the energy. |
| 4. Setup | "We're going to look at 3 [things], find what's wrong with each, and fix them. With insights you can apply to your own [thing]." | Setup ties review to viewer's own application. |
| 5. Transition | "First up is [contestant 1]" | Direct, gets to the work. |
| 6. Credibility line | Often woven into the SETUP ("I've reviewed 200+ of these") or the FIX itself ("here's what I'd do, having coached X people through this") | Credibility lands as the fix lands. Proof in action. |

**The submission CTA also lives in the intro** (within the first 90 seconds). "If you want me to review your [thing] in a future video, sign up below. I'll send the submission form." This is non-optional. It's how the format compounds.

## The 10-step planner

1. **Ask for submissions** on community wall, drive sign-ups to email list.
2. **Email the form** to email list 24 hours later.
3. **Set goal.** Sales (recommended) or Emails.
4. **Thumbnails and title** based on the most universal problems found in submissions.
5. **Viewer's top 3 questions.** Based on title and thumbnail.
6. **Review submissions (messy plan).** Look at problems first, find the universal ones.
7. **Refine.** Pick max 3 contestants, max 3 problems per contestant.
8. **Conversion strategy.** Submission CTA mentioned 3x, first within 90 seconds.
9. **Write or present.** Either screen-record plus film simultaneously (Loom), or do review work offline plus write a script plus present to camera plus insert screenshots in post.
10. **Upload and track.**

## Submission funnel: the engine

The format depends on a steady supply of submissions. The funnel:

1. **Community wall post** with this template:
   > "Want to [insert result they want]? I'm going to do some free [thing] reviews on the channel where I'll show you how to fix [insert problem]. All you need to do to submit your [thing] for review is sign up to my email list below. I'll be emailing a private submission form to everyone on the list in 24 hours."

2. **Email the submission form** 24 hours later. Form fields:
   - Name
   - Submission link
   - "What's your biggest struggle right now?" (this is gold, it's market research)
   - Permission checkbox

Keep the form SHORT. Long forms kill submissions.

### NEVER call it a roast publicly

Word choice matters. "Free review" gets 1,000 submissions. "Free roast" gets 20. The audience hears "roast" and thinks public humiliation. Always call it a "review" in promotion. Internally, in production, we call it a roast, but never in the funnel.

### Small audience workaround

If the audience is too small to generate submissions, **roast famous people or brands instead.** Their material is public. You don't need their submission. Same format, different supply.

## Per-review structure

For each contestant:

1. **Show what they have** (the submission visual: their thumbnail, their script, their landing page, etc.)
2. **Show what's wrong** (problem identification: frank, kind, specific)
3. **Show the fix** (the actionable insight: do THIS, not THAT)
4. **Show the result** (what the fix would produce: a mockup, an example, a comparable winner)

The fix is where the gold is. Pointing out the problem is one thing. Showing the solution is where viewers convert.

## Common mistakes

- Calling it a roast in promotion (kills submissions)
- Picking obscure problems instead of universal ones (doesn't serve the broader audience)
- Going 10/10 mean (too mean equals unwatchable, brand damage)
- Showing the problem without the fix (proves nothing)
- Too many contestants per video (max 3)
- Too many problems per contestant (max 3)
- No submission CTA (the funnel breaks, future videos run dry)

## Output format

Two production options:
1. **Live screen-record plus camera** using Loom. Annotate screenshots in real-time while filming yourself reviewing
2. **Offline review plus scripted presentation.** Do the review work first, write a script, present to camera, insert screenshots in post

Most creators end up doing option 2 after trying option 1 once and finding it too time-consuming. Both work. Pick based on production speed.

## Conversion strategy by goal

### Goal = Sales (recommended)

- The fix IS the conversion ("if you want this for yourself, here's the link")
- Direct CTA at end ("Want me to fix yours? My program is here")
- Optionally embed mid-video CTA right after a strong fix
- End screen → past high-converting sales video

### Goal = Emails

- Submission CTA itself becomes the email funnel
- Lead magnet: "the 7 things I look for when reviewing [thing]." Distilled framework
- Mention 3x: first within 90 seconds, middle, end

## Title formulas

- "I [Reviewed/Fixed] [Number] [Thing]s. Here's What I Found"
- "[Number] [Things] That [Need Fix] (And How To Fix Them)"
- "Reviewing [Thing]s From My Subscribers. [Specific Insight]"
- "The [Number] Worst [Thing]s I've Seen This Month"
- "Why These [Number] [Things] Aren't Working (Fixes Inside)"

## Thumbnail strategies for this format

Lean into: **Cognitive Dissonance** (visual contrast: bad version vs fixed version), **Comparison** (before-and-after split), **Social Hacking** (when roasting famous brands or creators).

The thumbnail should hint at the fix, not just the problem. "Here's what's broken" alone doesn't pull clicks. "Here's what's broken AND what we did about it" does.

## End screen routing

- **Sales goal** → past high-converting sales video OR another high-fix roast
- **Emails goal** → past high-converting email video
- **Always** → another roast (drives binge for the format-loyal audience)

## Strategy for compounding results

- Run a roast every 2-4 weeks to keep submissions flowing
- Build a simple submission funnel (community post, email, form, review)
- Save problems and submissions as content fuel. The form's "biggest struggle" field gives you future content ideas
- The submission list IS a buyer list. These viewers self-identified as having the problem. Treat them as warm leads.

## What to skip

- Don't call it a roast publicly
- Don't review obscure problems
- Don't roast at 10/10 mean
- Don't skip the fix
- Don't run with a bare CTA. The submission funnel is the engine
