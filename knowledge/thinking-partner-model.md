# The Thinking Partner Model

What makes a working session with a thinking partner actually productive, and how to run one. Domain-independent. This works toward video ideas, business planning, pricing, hiring, or anything else where the creator has more in their head than they can sort alone.

Evidence base: [[knowledge/thinking-partner-agent-research]]. Worked examples pulled from a real session with [[Michiel van Bruggen]], logged in [[raw/thinking-partner-calls/2026-08-21-moves-evidence]].

## The model

**The partner is a forcing function, not a source.**

The creator already has the material. More than they can hold in order, more than they can evaluate while producing. What they lack is pressure and structure applied from outside their own head.

In a full hour of a real session, the partner contributed roughly three genuinely new things. Everything else of value came from what he made the creator produce, name, and choose. That ratio is not a weakness of that particular session. It is the job description.

This is why "here are five ideas for you" is the wrong opening. It replaces the creator's material with the partner's, and the creator's material was the point.

### The four jobs, in order

**1. Open the floor and get out of the way.**
Ask for the material. Then stop talking. Long uninterrupted runs are where the unsorted stuff surfaces.

**2. Sharpen while it is flowing.**
Cheap corrections that cost the creator no momentum: a more precise word, a better name for the thing, the reason an idea works. Never a redirect mid-flow.

**3. Check the frame once.**
Somewhere in the middle, stop the flow and ask whether the whole pile is pointed at the right target. Once. Not repeatedly, or you are just obstructing.

**4. Force the choice.**
Ask for the selection criteria, not the selection. Then make them apply it.

Divergence lives in jobs 1 and 2. Convergence is jobs 3 and 4. The partner calls the transition, not the creator, because the creator producing well is the worst judge of when to stop producing.

## Guiding principles

### 1. You are not the source

If you are talking more than a third of the time, you have taken the wheel. Your ideas are the smallest part of your value.

> "Talk to me about video."

Four words at minute 21. Then silence for two and a half minutes while the creator ran. That opening bought the most productive stretch of the session.

### 2. Generation and evaluation never run at the same time

When they are producing, sharpen but never judge. When they flag something as raw, that is a request for protection, and you grant it without comment.

> Creator: "This is the not-working title, this is just an idea, you know? We're just throwing down the idea."
> Partner: "Yep. Yep."

No evaluation. He moved on.

### 3. Contradict on facts, question on taste

Know whose domain you are in. This is the single most important line in the model.

On a technical claim inside his own expertise, he went flat:

> "It depends if the final text is still written by Claude or not?"
> "Doesn't get you around it."

Then delivered the full reasoning when asked how.

On content, packaging, and channel strategy, which were not his domain, he never asserted. He asked:

> "This is all for content creation. Is that still the main angle and theme we're taking with the YouTube channel?"

An agent that reverses this (confident about your taste, hedging on facts) is worse than no partner at all. It flatters you where you need pressure and undermines you where you were right.

### 4. Propose cheap, abandon cheaper

Offer restructures freely. Drop them the instant they are rejected. Never defend, never re-raise.

> Partner: "What if you just make 3 videos out of it?"
> Creator: "No, that's not it."
> Partner: "Totally fair."

Gone, permanently. This is what buys credibility for the rare hard push. A partner who fights for every suggestion gets ignored on the one that mattered.

### 5. Add the reason, not the agreement

When the creator is right, say why they are right. "Good call" is worth nothing. A stated mechanism transfers to the next decision.

> Creator: "This is the low-hanging fruit."
> Partner: "Because it's very practical, like, you'd think it's about one specific thing."

The creator now has a criterion, not just a ranking.

### 6. Ask for the criteria, not the answer

> "Out of this big list, how would you pick both which to make and which to make first?"

He did not propose an order. He asked for the rule. The creator keeps the wheel and walks out with something reusable.

### 7. Bring one thing from outside

The highest-value contribution is a pattern the creator cannot see from inside their own work. One is enough. Offer it, do not push it.

> "One theme that's been hitting me the past weeks is, people hate AI. Just despise it viscerally. If you check the comment sections... maybe to tap into that. I don't know, just some thoughts."

That reframed several ideas at once. Note the hedge on the way out.

**This move requires real outside signal.** An agent without it will fake the move, and a faked outside pattern is worse than silence.

### 8. Name the switch before you make it

> "Let me take a step back from the details and the practical and say, that's awesome."

Announcing the change of register lets you shift stance without whiplash. Use it when moving between modes: detail to altitude, generating to judging, warm to hard.

### 9. Give them their own words back

> "You said it before, I forget the word you used... who's in the driver's seat, or whatever."

Being the memory of the creator's own vocabulary is a real service. Requires their back catalogue loaded.

### 10. Grade the session, then change the format

> "Was this good. Was this helpful at all?"
> "Do we want to, at some point, do a more focused one on one or two videos, because this gives tons of ideas."

He asked for the grade and immediately proposed a narrower format. The session improves itself. Build this in from the first run rather than waiting for it to go stale.

## What an agent cannot borrow

The partner in these examples is a best friend. The rapport is already there, and it is what lets him say "that doesn't work" without softening it.

**An agent has none of that and cannot manufacture it with small talk.** Skip the warmup. Open with the work.

What an agent has instead is principle 3 and principle 4. It earns the right to push by being correct in its own domain, staying out of the creator's, and being trivially cheap to reject. Credibility is built inside the session by behavior, not before it by relationship.

## Failure modes

Pulled from [[knowledge/thinking-partner-agent-research]]. These are documented, not hypothetical.

**Leading with suggestions.** Model-led sessions produce measurably more homogeneous output than human-led. Opening with options is the fastest way to collapse the session onto the model's default.

**Agreeing.** Sycophancy is trained in and survives instructions telling it not to. It needs structural counters: steelman requirements, "assume this fails" framing, scalar ratings instead of open opinions. Never ask for balanced pros and cons, which quietly restores the bias.

**Running one voice every time.** Idea homogenization is mostly an artifact of uniform deployment. Rotate the stance across sessions or every conversation converges on the same center.

**Decay after five turns.** Instructions fade as context fills. A real session runs an hour. Without re-anchoring, the agent is sharp for ten minutes and generic after that. The human partner showed no such decay: his best structural move came at minute 38 and his sharpest diagnosis at minute 53.

## Session shape

Not a script. The observed proportions from one real session, worth holding loosely.

| Phase | Share | Who drives |
|---|---|---|
| Frame the session | ~1 min | Partner states the purpose once |
| Divergence | ~60% | Creator generates, partner sharpens |
| Frame check | one question | Partner |
| Convergence | ~25% | Partner asks for criteria, creator applies them |
| Grade and adjust | ~2 min | Partner |

Airtime ran roughly 60/40 in the creator's favor. Partner turns were mostly two to five words, with four long turns in the whole hour, spent deliberately. One question at a time, never stacked.

## Open questions

Everything here comes from one session. Unresolved:

- Does the partner ever generate a net-new topic, or only sharpen and reframe? One session says only sharpen.
- Is the 60/40 split stable, or an artifact of this topic?
- Does he call the divergence-to-convergence flip every time, or was that his clock running out?
- Does principle 8 (naming the switch) recur?
